Q: Outline how urbanisation alters the water cycle in a drainage basin. (4 marks)
A: Model answer: Concrete and tarmac are impermeable, so infiltration falls (1) and overland flow rises (d). Drains carry water quickly to channels (1), shortening lag time and raising peak discharge (d). Less vegetation means less interception and transpiration (1).\nMarking points: Point marked, AO1 only. 1 mark per valid change, (d) for developing it. Max 3 if only one factor. Max 1 for a list of factors with no development.\nCommon trap: Writing about flood damage to people instead of changes to stores and flows, or listing factors with no development.
Q: Explain the concept of positive feedback in the carbon cycle. (4 marks)
A: Model answer: Positive feedback amplifies an initial change (1). Warming thaws permafrost (1), which releases stored carbon as CO2 and methane (d). This adds to the greenhouse effect, causing more warming and more thaw (d).\nMarking points: Point marked, AO1. 1 for an accurate definition, then marks for an example developed as a linked loop.\nCommon trap: Describing negative feedback (which dampens change), or giving a definition with no example of the loop.
Q: Outline the processes that transfer carbon from the atmosphere into the ocean store. (4 marks)
A: Model answer: CO2 dissolves into surface seawater (1), more readily in cold water (d). Phytoplankton take in CO2 by photosynthesis (1). When they die, carbon sinks to the deep ocean and sea-floor sediments, the biological pump (d).\nMarking points: Point marked, AO1. Credit each named process plus development. Max 3 if only one process.\nCommon trap: Drifting away from the key term, here writing about acidification effects on coral instead of transfer processes. Off-term points score nothing.
Q: A drainage basin has annual precipitation of 900 mm, evapotranspiration of 450 mm and runoff of 380 mm. Calculate the change in storage and state what it shows.
A: Model answer: Water balance P = Q + E +/- change in storage. 900 - 450 - 380 = +70 mm. Storage increased by 70 mm, so the basin has a water surplus that year.\nMarking points: Correct equation, correct working, units (mm), and a correct comment on surplus or deficit.\nCommon trap: Subtracting in the wrong order or dropping the sign, so a surplus is called a deficit.
Q: Figure shows monthly precipitation (bars) and mean river discharge (line) for a UK drainage basin over one year. Analyse the data shown. (6 marks)
A: Model answer: Overall, discharge is highest in winter, when precipitation is also highest, so the two are positively linked. Quote the peak months and values, and calculate a difference or ratio between the wettest and driest months. Note any anomaly, such as a wet summer month with little rise in discharge.\nMarking points: AO3 only. Level 2 (4-6): clear analysis, appropriate use of data, clear connections between the data sets. Level 1: basic, little data.\nCommon trap: Explaining why, such as summer evapotranspiration. Explanation is AO2 and gains no credit here.
Q: Using a figure showing land-use change in a river catchment and your own knowledge, assess how the changes affect flood risk. (6 marks)
A: Model answer: The figure shows woodland replaced by housing and arable fields near the river. Less interception and infiltration plus drains mean faster runoff, a shorter lag time and a higher peak. Urban land beside the channel matters most, as it is close and impermeable. Judgement: flood risk rises, most in the lower catchment.\nMarking points: AO1 2, AO2 4. Level 2 needs knowledge applied clearly to the resource and a judgement. No specific place knowledge needed.\nCommon trap: Only re-describing the figure (AO3), or reciting flood knowledge with no link to the figure (AO1 only).
Q: Assess the extent to which wildfires disrupt the carbon cycle. (9 marks)
A: Model answer: Combustion moves carbon from biomass to the atmosphere in hours. In fire-adapted ecosystems regrowth re-absorbs it over years to decades. Peat and soil fires release long-term stores that are slow to recover. Warming brings more frequent fires, a positive feedback. Judgement: disruption is short term unless fires burn peat or become more frequent.\nMarking points: AO1 4, AO2 5. This links Hazards to Water and carbon. Level 3 needs detailed carbon knowledge.\nCommon trap: Narrating a fire event without carbon stores, flows and timescales.
Q: With reference to a tropical rainforest you have studied, assess the extent to which human activity has changed its water and carbon cycles. (20 marks)
A: Model answer: Intro: in the Amazon, deforestation is the main driver. Carbon: burning shifts carbon from biomass and soil to the atmosphere. Water: less transpiration and recycled rainfall, more runoff. Link the cycles: a drier forest burns more, a positive feedback. Counterweight: natural droughts and reforestation (Brazil's target: 12 million hectares by 2030). Conclusion.\nMarking points: AO1 10, AO2 10. Level 4: case study detail throughout, scale and time, a firm conclusion.\nCommon trap: Ignoring natural factors, or describing the forest without saying how much the cycles changed.
Q: Assess the effectiveness of human interventions designed to reduce carbon transfers to the atmosphere. (20 marks)
A: Model answer: Set a position, then judge each intervention by scale, speed, cost and politics. Afforestation is cheap but slow to store carbon. Carbon capture and storage works only at a small scale so far. Renewable energy cuts emissions at the source. Global agreements such as Paris 2015 depend on nations keeping their pledges. Conclusion: decide which works best and why.\nMarking points: AO1 10, AO2 10. Level 3 and 4 need evaluation and a supported conclusion. Recall alone caps at Level 2.\nCommon trap: Listing strategies without judging them, or drifting into the causes of climate change.
Q: Outline the processes leading to the formation of a spit. (4 marks)
A: Model answer: Longshore drift moves sediment along the coast in the direction of the prevailing wind (1). Where the coastline changes direction, sediment is deposited into open water (1) and builds a ridge (d). Wave refraction and changing winds curve the end into a hook (1). Saltmarsh forms in the sheltered water behind (d).\nMarking points: Point marked, AO1. A logical sequence of processes earns the marks. Max 3 if only one process.\nCommon trap: Describing what the spit looks like instead of the processes that formed it.
Q: Outline the characteristics of a wave-cut platform. (4 marks)
A: Model answer: A gently sloping rocky surface at the foot of a cliff (1), extending out to sea (d). It is covered at high tide and exposed at low tide (1). The surface is uneven, with rock pools and ridges along weaker joints (1). A notch or cliff line marks its landward edge (d).\nMarking points: Point marked, AO1. Credit features that make the landform distinct. Formation is credited only where it overlaps with characteristics.\nCommon trap: Writing about how it formed instead of its characteristics.
Q: Figure shows cliff erosion rates (m per year) at survey points along a coastline, with a geology map. Analyse the data shown. (6 marks)
A: Model answer: Overall, erosion is fastest on the clay sections and slowest on the chalk headland. Quote the highest and lowest rates and how many times faster one is. Use the scale and direction: rates increase towards the south. Point out anomalies, such as a fast-eroding clay site next to slow ones. Connect the rates to the geology map.\nMarking points: AO3 only. Level 2: clear analysis, accurate data use and clear connections between figures. Level 1: basic.\nCommon trap: Explaining why clay erodes fast (AO2, no credit), or treating the two figures separately with no link.
Q: Pebble lengths (cm) at 8 sites on a beach are 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9. Calculate the standard deviation and evaluate how useful it is for this data. (6 marks)
A: Model answer: Mean = 40 / 8 = 5. Squared deviations: 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16 = 32. 32 / 8 = 4, and the square root is 2.0 cm. 6 of 8 values (75%) lie within 1 SD (3-7 cm). The SD shows the spread around the mean, but it is more useful when compared with another beach, and n = 8 is small.\nMarking points: AO3. Marks for an accurate table and the final calculation (method credit even if the answer is wrong), then up to 3 for evaluation points.\nCommon trap: Doing the calculation but never evaluating how useful the technique is.
Q: Using only two maps of the same coastline from different dates, evaluate their relative usefulness in showing coastal change. (6 marks)

The time gap between the maps is long, so the rate of change is hidden. Neither map shows height, so cliff retreat cannot be judged. Overall, Map A is more useful.\nMarking points: AO3. Evaluate the presentation: scale, keys, dates, accuracy and missing information. Reach a judgement on relative usefulness.\nCommon trap: Analysing the change shown instead of judging the sources, which earns little credit.
Q: Using a figure showing a proposed managed realignment scheme and your own knowledge, assess the sustainability of this approach. (6 marks)
A: Model answer: The figure shows an embankment breached to flood farmland and create saltmarsh. Saltmarsh absorbs wave energy, adds habitat and stores carbon. Long-term costs are lower than maintaining walls. Against this, farmland is lost and landowners need compensation. Judgement: sustainable if the community supports it.\nMarking points: AO1 2, AO2 4. Level 2 needs knowledge applied to the resource and a judgement. No case study recital is needed.\nCommon trap: Reciting a learned case study, or only basic hard v soft engineering, instead of engaging with the figure.
Q: Assess the relative importance of geology in the development of landforms on high-energy erosional coastlines. (20 marks)

Q: With reference to a coastal landscape beyond the UK, evaluate human responses to the risks it presents. (20 marks)

Q: Assess the impact of sea level change on the development of coastal landscapes. (20 marks)
A: Model answer: Separate eustatic, isostatic and tectonic change. Emergence leaves raised beaches and marine platforms. Submergence creates rias, fjords and Dalmatian coasts. Sea level rise moves erosion and deposition inland, threatening spits and saltmarsh. Weigh this against waves, geology and human management. Conclusion: judge how far it shapes landscapes compared with other factors.\nMarking points: AO1 10, AO2 10. Level 4 needs links from process to landform and an awareness of time.\nCommon trap: Describing the impacts of sea level rise on people and barely reaching the landforms.
Q: Outline the processes involved in the formation of a deep-sea trench. (4 marks)
A: Model answer: At a destructive margin, oceanic crust meets another plate (1). The denser oceanic plate is subducted (1), pulled down by slab pull (d). The downward flexing plate forms a long, deep trench parallel to the margin (d).\nMarking points: Point marked, AO1. Credit a correct sequence starting from convergence and subduction.\nCommon trap: Confusing trenches with ocean ridges (constructive margins), which earns almost no credit.
Q: Outline the Park model of human response to hazards. (4 marks)
A: Model answer: A graph of quality of life against time, before and after a hazard event (1). Quality of life drops sharply during the event and the relief phase (1). It recovers through rehabilitation and reconstruction (d). The depth of the fall and the level of recovery depend on preparedness and development (d).\nMarking points: Point marked, AO1. Credit the axes, the phases and what controls the curve's shape.\nCommon trap: Describing the Hazard Management Cycle instead, or narrating one event without the model.

Q: Using a figure showing a volcano, its hazard zones, valleys and nearby settlements, and your own knowledge, assess the challenges of managing volcanic hazards in this area. (9 marks)

Q: To what extent is prediction more important than preparedness in reducing the impacts of tropical storms? (9 marks)
A: Model answer: Satellite tracking gives days of warning, so prediction is the starting point. Warnings save lives only when people can act on them through shelters, evacuation plans and transport. Hurricane Katrina in 2005 was well forecast, but many residents without cars stayed. Judgement: prediction is necessary but preparedness decides the outcome.\nMarking points: AO1 4, AO2 5. Level 3 needs a clear view on the extent, supported by examples.\nCommon trap: Contrasting two storms in detail without answering the "to what extent" question.
Q: Assess the role of international organisations in reducing the impacts of tectonic hazards. (9 marks)
A: Model answer: The UN's Sendai Framework of 2015 sets disaster-risk targets, and the Red Cross and World Bank fund relief and rebuilding. After the 2010 Haiti earthquake, aid was large but poorly coordinated. Building codes and national planning, as in Japan, often matter more. Judgement: international help is valuable but secondary to national action.\nMarking points: AO1 4, AO2 5. This links Hazards to Global governance. Level 3 needs named organisations and an assessment.\nCommon trap: A generic account of "aid" with no named organisation and no judgement.
Q: 'Level of development is the most important factor in determining the impact of earthquakes.' To what extent do you agree? (20 marks)
A: Model answer: Position: development matters, but physical factors and governance also count. Haiti 2010 (magnitude 7.0) shows how poverty and weak buildings raise deaths. In Tohoku 2011, a rich country still suffered huge losses from the tsunami. In Turkey-Syria 2023, poorly enforced building codes mattered. Also weigh depth, distance and secondary hazards. Conclusion: a clear, ranked judgement.\nMarking points: AO1 10, AO2 10. Level 4 needs detailed case studies used to argue throughout.\nCommon trap: Comparing two case studies in sequence without a sustained argument about "most important".
Q: Assess the extent to which human responses enable the continued occupation of a multi-hazardous environment you have studied. (20 marks)

Q: Outline how urban areas affect wind. (4 marks)

Q: Which word set describes Level 4 work in the AQA Geography NEA levels?
A: Detailed, effective, thorough, complete, well-developed
Q: Which word set describes Level 2 work in the AQA Geography NEA levels?
A: Intermittent, partial, some, implicit, imprecise, inconsistent
Q: How do you write an NEA title and introduction that reaches Level 4 in Area 1?
A: Model answer: A focused, testable question with a theoretical basis and a named location, such as "To what extent does pebble size decrease in the direction of longshore drift at a named beach?". Link it to the spec (coastal transport). Review literature and theory, and compare with other studies.\nMarking points: Area 1, 10 marks AO3. Level 4 (9-10): question effectively identified and completely referenced to the spec; theoretical and comparative contexts well understood and stated.\nCommon trap: A broad, generalised title, or a literature section that is never linked to the question.
Q: How do you write the methods section of an NEA to reach Level 4 in Area 2?
A: Model answer: Describe each method, with its sampling (systematic every 50 m along a transect, stratified by land use), sample size, frequency and timing. Justify each choice: why it is representative, accurate and safe, and how it answers the aim. Mention a pilot study and the risks.\nMarking points: Area 2, 15 marks AO3. Level 4 (12-15): detailed range of approaches including sampling, thorough and well-reasoned justification, detailed implementation.\nCommon trap: Describing what you did without justifying why, which is "some justification" (Level 2).
Q: How do you reach Level 4 in Area 3 (Methods of critical analysis) of the NEA?
A: Model answer: Choose techniques that suit the data, such as Spearman's rank for a relationship or chi-squared for a distribution, with a significance level. Interpret the results against theory and expected patterns. Comment on accuracy, representativeness and anomalies, and say what the fieldwork taught you.\nMarking points: Area 3, 20 marks (AO2 6, AO3 14). Level 4 (15-20): effective techniques, thorough critical examination of data, effective use of theory to order observations.\nCommon trap: Presenting many graphs with only description, never interrogating the data or linking to theory.
Q: In an NEA, 6 sites give a Spearman's rank sum of d squared of 8. Calculate rs and say how to judge whether it is significant.
A: Model answer: rs = 1 - (6 x 8) / (6^3 - 6) = 1 - 48/210 = 1 - 0.229 = 0.77. This is a strong positive correlation. Compare 0.77 with the critical value for n = 6 at the 0.05 significance level in a table.\nMarking points: Correct formula, correct working, the direction and strength, then a significance test against critical values.\nCommon trap: Using n squared instead of n cubed minus n, or stating a strong correlation as proof without testing significance.
Q: How do you write NEA conclusions and evaluation that reach Level 4 in Area 4?
A: Model answer: Summarise the results for each aim, then answer the title with a well-argued case. Relate the findings to the wider context and theory. Evaluate the methods, accuracy and limitations, the ethical dimensions and ideas for further research.\nMarking points: Area 4, 15 marks AO3. Level 4 (12-15): thorough, logical write-up with a range of presentation methods, effective evaluation, complete link to wider context, thorough ethics.\nCommon trap: A conclusion that repeats the results without answering the question, or leaving out ethics.
Q: How should ethics appear in an AQA Geography NEA?
A: Model answer: Explain the consent and anonymity of questionnaire respondents, permission to use private land, avoiding damage to sites, and fair representation of communities in how data is presented.\nMarking points: Area 4, AO3: Level 4 needs a thorough understanding of the ethical dimensions of field research.\nCommon trap: A single line on safety (a risk assessment) mistaken for ethics.
Q: How should data be presented in an NEA to score well in Area 4?
A: Model answer: Use a range of methods suited to each data type, such as located proportional symbols on maps, scatter graphs with best-fit lines and annotated photos. Each one is titled, labelled, scaled and referred to in the text.\nMarking points: Area 4 (AO3 strand 3): Level 4 needs a thorough, clear and logical write-up using a range of presentation methods.\nCommon trap: Many similar bar charts or decorative graphs that are never analysed in the text.
