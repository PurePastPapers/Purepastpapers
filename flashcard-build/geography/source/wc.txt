## AQA_GEOG_3.1.1.1 | content
Q: In systems theory, what is a system?
A: A set of linked stores (components) with flows (transfers) of matter or energy between them
Q: What is the term for a system that receives inputs from, and loses outputs to, its surroundings?
A: An open system
Q: What is the term for a system where energy crosses the boundary but matter does not?
A: A closed system
Q: At the global scale, is the water cycle an open or a closed system?
A: Closed (no water enters or leaves; energy does)
Q: Is a drainage basin an open or a closed system?
A: Open (water enters as precipitation and leaves as runoff and evapotranspiration)
Q: At the global scale, is the carbon cycle an open or a closed system?
A: Closed (carbon is recycled within the Earth system)
Q: In a system, what is an input?
A: Matter or energy that enters the system from outside
Q: In a system, what is an output?
A: Matter or energy that leaves the system
Q: In a system, what is a store?
A: A part of the system where matter or energy is held for a time
Q: In a system, what is a flow (transfer)?
A: The movement of matter or energy from one store to another
Q: Which energy source drives evaporation and most of the water cycle?
A: Solar energy (the sun)
Q: Which force drives water downhill through a drainage basin and makes precipitation fall?
A: Gravity
Q: What is the term for a system state where inputs and outputs balance so the stores stay roughly constant over time?
A: Dynamic equilibrium
Q: Why is dynamic equilibrium called "dynamic"?
A: Flows keep happening, but the overall balance between them stays the same
Q: What type of feedback reverses a change and returns a system towards equilibrium?
A: Negative feedback
Q: What type of feedback amplifies a change and moves a system further from equilibrium?
A: Positive feedback
Q: In a negative carbon feedback, how does more atmospheric CO2 lead to more carbon being stored?
A: Plants photosynthesise faster and take up more CO2 (CO2 fertilisation)
Q: Which type of event can suddenly upset the dynamic equilibrium of a drainage basin?
A: An extreme event such as a storm, flood or drought
Q: What is the term for a critical point beyond which a system changes to a new state and does not return?
A: A tipping point (threshold)
Q: What is the term for the area that a system covers, which separates it from its surroundings?
A: The system boundary
Q: In a drainage basin system, which feature forms the boundary?
A: The watershed
Q: In systems terms, what is a sink?
A: A store that takes in more of a substance (e.g. carbon) than it releases
Q: In systems terms, what is a source?
A: A store that releases more of a substance than it takes in

## AQA_GEOG_3.1.1.2 | content
Q: Which of the four water stores (lithosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, atmosphere) holds the most water?
A: The hydrosphere (the oceans)
Q: Which store holds most of Earth's fresh water?
A: The cryosphere (ice sheets and glaciers)
Q: After ice, which store holds the next largest share of Earth's fresh water?
A: Groundwater (in the lithosphere)
Q: Which of the four main water stores holds the smallest volume of water?
A: The atmosphere
Q: What is the cryosphere?
A: The parts of Earth where water is frozen (ice sheets, glaciers, sea ice, permafrost, snow)
Q: What is the lithosphere's water store mainly made of?
A: Groundwater and soil water held in rocks and soil
Q: What is residence time in the water cycle?
A: The average time a water molecule spends in a store
Q: Why does water in the atmosphere have a much shorter residence time than water in the oceans?
A: The atmosphere's store is tiny compared with the rapid flows of evaporation and precipitation
Q: What is evaporation?
A: The change of liquid water into water vapour
Q: What is the term for the energy absorbed when water evaporates, which is released again on condensation?
A: Latent heat
Q: Which factor increases evaporation by removing saturated air from above a water surface?
A: Wind
Q: Why does evaporation increase as air temperature rises?
A: Warm air holds more water vapour and gives more energy for evaporation
Q: What is condensation?
A: The change of water vapour into liquid water droplets
Q: What is the dew point?
A: The temperature at which air becomes saturated and condensation begins
Q: What do tiny particles such as dust, salt and smoke provide for cloud formation?
A: Condensation nuclei for water droplets to form on
Q: What is the term for cooling of rising air caused by its expansion at lower pressure, with no heat lost to surroundings?
A: Adiabatic cooling
Q: At what rate does unsaturated (dry) air cool as it rises?
A: About 9.8°C per 1,000 m (the dry adiabatic lapse rate)
Q: Why does saturated air cool more slowly than dry air as it rises?
A: Condensation releases latent heat, which partly offsets the cooling
Q: What is the term for the rate at which temperature falls with height in still air?
A: The environmental lapse rate
Q: When rising air stays warmer than the air around it, what is the atmosphere said to be?
A: Unstable (air keeps rising, forming towering clouds)
Q: What is the term for rainfall caused by the ground heating air, which rises, cools and condenses?
A: Convectional rainfall
Q: What is the term for rainfall caused when moist air is forced to rise over high ground?
A: Relief (orographic) rainfall
Q: What is the term for rainfall caused when warm air is forced to rise over colder, denser air?
A: Frontal rainfall
Q: What is the term for the drier area on the downwind (leeward) side of mountains?
A: A rain shadow
Q: Why is the leeward side of mountains drier?
A: Descending air warms, so water does not condense
Q: Which type of rainfall gives the UK most of its rain, through mid-latitude depressions?
A: Frontal rainfall
Q: What is sublimation?
A: The change of ice directly into water vapour without melting
Q: What is the term for water vapour changing directly into ice, as in frost?
A: Deposition
Q: In a glacier, what is accumulation?
A: Inputs of snow and ice to the glacier store
Q: In a glacier, what is ablation?
A: Loss of ice by melting, sublimation or calving
Q: What is calving?
A: Blocks of ice breaking off the front of a glacier or ice sheet into water
Q: In which season does ablation usually exceed accumulation on glaciers?
A: Summer
Q: What happens to global sea level when ice sheets grow in a glacial period?
A: It falls, as water is moved from the oceans into the cryosphere
Q: What is a drainage basin?
A: The area of land drained by a river and its tributaries
Q: What is a watershed?
A: The boundary (usually a ridge of high land) between two drainage basins
Q: What is a confluence?
A: The point where two rivers join
Q: What is a tributary?
A: A smaller stream that joins a larger river
Q: What is the main input to a drainage basin?
A: Precipitation
Q: What is interception?
A: Precipitation caught and held by vegetation before it reaches the ground
Q: What is the term for intercepted water that evaporates from leaves without reaching the ground?
A: Interception loss
Q: What is the term for water flowing down the trunks and stems of plants to the ground?
A: Stemflow
Q: What is the term for water dripping from leaves to the ground?
A: Throughfall
Q: Why is interception lower in winter in deciduous woodland?
A: The trees have lost their leaves
Q: What is surface storage?
A: Water held on the ground surface in puddles, ponds and lakes
Q: What is infiltration?
A: The movement of water from the surface into the soil
Q: What is the infiltration capacity of a soil?
A: The maximum rate at which water can soak into the soil
Q: Which unit is infiltration rate usually measured in?
A: Millimetres per hour (mm/hr)
Q: Why do sandy soils have a higher infiltration capacity than clay soils?
A: They have larger pore spaces between particles
Q: What is soil water storage?
A: Water held in the pores of the soil
Q: What is throughflow?
A: Water moving downslope through the soil towards the river
Q: What is percolation?
A: Water moving down from the soil into the rock below
Q: What is groundwater?
A: Water stored in the pores and joints of rock below the water table
Q: What is the water table?
A: The upper level of the saturated zone in the ground
Q: What is an aquifer?
A: A permeable rock that stores and transmits groundwater (e.g. chalk or sandstone)
Q: What is the term for the slow flow of groundwater through rock to the river?
A: Groundwater flow
Q: What is groundwater recharge?
A: Refilling of groundwater stores by water percolating down
Q: What is channel storage?
A: Water held in the river channel itself
Q: What is channel flow?
A: The movement of water along the river channel
Q: What is overland flow?
A: Water flowing across the ground surface towards a river
Q: What is the term for overland flow caused when rainfall intensity exceeds the infiltration capacity?
A: Infiltration-excess (Hortonian) overland flow
Q: What is the term for overland flow caused when the soil is already full of water?
A: Saturation-excess overland flow
Q: What is runoff in a drainage basin?
A: All the water leaving the basin as river flow (surface and subsurface)
Q: What is transpiration?
A: Loss of water vapour from plants through pores (stomata) in their leaves
Q: What is evapotranspiration?
A: The combined loss of water by evaporation and transpiration
Q: What is the term for the amount of evapotranspiration that would happen if water supply were unlimited?
A: Potential evapotranspiration
Q: What is the water balance equation for a drainage basin?
A: P = Q + E ± ΔS (precipitation = runoff + evapotranspiration ± change in storage)
Q: In the water balance equation, what does Q stand for?
A: Runoff (river discharge)
Q: What is the term for a period when evapotranspiration exceeds precipitation and plants draw on soil water?
A: Soil moisture utilisation
Q: What is the term for the shortage of soil water when evapotranspiration has used up the soil store?
A: Soil moisture deficit
Q: What is the term for autumn and winter rain refilling the soil water store?
A: Soil moisture recharge
Q: What is the term for a period when soil is full and extra precipitation becomes runoff?
A: Water surplus
Q: In the UK, in which season is a soil moisture deficit most likely?
A: Summer
Q: What is river discharge?
A: The volume of water passing a point on a river per second
Q: In which unit is river discharge measured?
A: Cumecs (cubic metres per second)
Q: How is discharge calculated?
A: Cross-sectional area × velocity
Q: What is a flood (storm) hydrograph?
A: A graph of river discharge over time after a rainfall event
Q: What is lag time on a flood hydrograph?
A: The time between peak rainfall and peak discharge
Q: What is the rising limb of a hydrograph?
A: The part of the graph where discharge increases after the rain
Q: What is the falling (recession) limb of a hydrograph?
A: The part of the graph where discharge decreases after the peak
Q: What is baseflow?
A: The normal flow of a river, fed mainly by groundwater
Q: What is stormflow (quickflow)?
A: Discharge from overland flow and fast throughflow after rain
Q: What is peak discharge?
A: The highest discharge reached after a storm
Q: What is bankfull discharge?
A: The discharge at which the river just fills its channel (any more and it floods)
Q: What is a "flashy" hydrograph?
A: One with a short lag time, steep rising limb and high peak
Q: What does a subdued (flat) hydrograph show?
A: A long lag time and a low peak discharge
Q: What is antecedent rainfall?
A: Rain that fell in the days before a storm, leaving the ground wetter
Q: How does saturated ground from antecedent rainfall affect a flood hydrograph?
A: Less infiltration, so more overland flow, a shorter lag time and higher peak
Q: How does a circular drainage basin affect lag time compared with an elongated one?
A: Shorter lag time, as water from all parts reaches the river at a similar time
Q: How does a small drainage basin affect lag time?
A: Shorter lag time, as water has less distance to travel
Q: What is drainage density?
A: The total length of streams per unit area of the basin (km per km²)
Q: How does a high drainage density affect a flood hydrograph?
A: Water reaches the main river faster, giving a shorter lag time and higher peak
Q: How do steep slopes affect a flood hydrograph?
A: Faster overland flow, so a shorter lag time and higher peak
Q: How does impermeable rock such as granite or clay affect a flood hydrograph?
A: Little percolation, so more overland flow and a flashier hydrograph
Q: How does permeable rock such as chalk affect a flood hydrograph?
A: Water percolates to groundwater, giving a long lag time and low peak
Q: How does dense forest cover affect a flood hydrograph?
A: More interception and transpiration, so a longer lag time and lower peak
Q: How does urbanisation affect a flood hydrograph?
A: Impermeable surfaces and drains speed runoff, so lag time falls and peak rises
Q: How do storm drains in towns affect river response?
A: They carry rainwater quickly to rivers, shortening lag time
Q: How does frozen ground affect a flood hydrograph?
A: It acts as an impermeable surface, increasing overland flow
Q: Why does rapid snowmelt cause flooding?
A: Large volumes of water are released at once, often onto frozen or saturated ground
Q: How does high rainfall intensity affect overland flow?
A: Rain arrives faster than the infiltration capacity, so overland flow increases
Q: What is the term for an approach that works with natural processes (e.g. leaky dams, tree planting) to cut flood peaks?
A: Natural flood management
Q: How does ploughing up and down a slope affect runoff?
A: Furrows act as channels, speeding overland flow
Q: How does soil compaction by heavy machinery or livestock affect the water cycle?
A: It reduces infiltration and increases overland flow
Q: How does field drainage on farmland affect a hydrograph?
A: Water reaches rivers faster, shortening lag time
Q: How does irrigation affect the water cycle?
A: It removes water from rivers or groundwater and raises evaporation from fields
Q: How does deforestation affect a drainage basin's water cycle?
A: Less interception and transpiration, so more runoff and higher flood risk
Q: What is water abstraction?
A: Removing water from rivers, lakes or groundwater for human use
Q: What happens to the water table if groundwater is abstracted faster than it is recharged?
A: It falls
Q: How do reservoirs and dams affect a river's discharge downstream?
A: They store water and regulate flow, reducing flood peaks and low flows
Q: How do seasonal changes in the UK affect river discharge?
A: Discharge is usually higher in winter (more rain, less evapotranspiration)
Q: How does a storm event change drainage basin stores in the short term?
A: It fills soil, surface and channel stores and raises discharge
Q: What happens to river discharge when a drought lowers soil and groundwater stores?
A: Discharge falls as baseflow is reduced
Q: Which long-term natural change moved large amounts of water from the oceans into ice stores?
A: Glacial periods (ice ages)
Q: How does global warming affect the cryosphere store?
A: It shrinks, as ice melts and water moves into the oceans
Q: Why does warmer air lead to more intense rainfall?
A: Warmer air holds more water vapour (about 7% more per 1°C)
Q: What is the term for a natural climate pattern of warming in the eastern Pacific that alters rainfall patterns around the world?
A: El Niño (ENSO)

## AQA_GEOG_3.1.1.3 | content
Q: Which of the carbon stores is by far the largest?
A: The lithosphere (sedimentary rocks such as limestone, and fossil fuels)
Q: Which is the second largest carbon store after the lithosphere?
A: The oceans (hydrosphere), mostly as dissolved inorganic carbon in the deep ocean
Q: In which form is most carbon in the oceans stored?
A: Dissolved inorganic carbon (bicarbonate and carbonate ions)
Q: In which form is most carbon in the atmosphere stored?
A: Carbon dioxide (CO2), with smaller amounts of methane (CH4)
Q: Where is carbon stored in the cryosphere?
A: In permafrost soils and in ice (as trapped gas)
Q: Where is carbon stored in the biosphere?
A: In living plants and animals and in dead organic matter
Q: On land, which holds more carbon: soils or vegetation?
A: Soils
Q: Why do cold high-latitude soils store large amounts of carbon?
A: Low temperatures slow decomposition, so organic matter builds up (e.g. peat)
Q: What is peat?
A: Partly decomposed plant matter that builds up in waterlogged, acidic, cold conditions
Q: What is photosynthesis?
A: Plants use sunlight to turn CO2 and water into glucose and oxygen
Q: What is the word equation for photosynthesis?
A: Carbon dioxide + water (+ light) → glucose + oxygen
Q: What is respiration?
A: Organisms break down glucose for energy, releasing CO2 and water
Q: What is decomposition?
A: The breakdown of dead organic matter by bacteria, fungi and detritivores, releasing CO2
Q: What are organisms such as worms and woodlice that feed on dead organic matter called?
A: Detritivores
Q: Why is decomposition fastest in hot, wet climates?
A: Warmth and moisture increase microbial activity
Q: What gas does decomposition release in waterlogged, oxygen-poor conditions?
A: Methane (CH4)
Q: What is combustion?
A: Burning of organic matter or fossil fuels, releasing CO2 to the atmosphere
Q: What is carbon sequestration?
A: The capture and long-term storage of carbon from the atmosphere
Q: What is net primary productivity (NPP)?
A: The carbon fixed by photosynthesis minus the carbon lost by plant respiration
Q: What is gross primary productivity (GPP)?
A: The total carbon fixed by plants through photosynthesis
Q: What is the term for the gradual change in plant communities over time on a site?
A: Succession
Q: What is a sere?
A: One complete sequence of succession from bare ground to climax community
Q: How does carbon storage change as a sere develops?
A: It increases, as biomass and soil organic matter build up
Q: What is the final, stable stage of succession called?
A: The climax community
Q: At the plant scale, where does a plant store the carbon it fixes?
A: In its leaves, stems, trunk and roots (biomass)
Q: How does carbon pass from plants to animals?
A: Animals eat plants (consumption along food chains)
Q: What is the term for dead leaves and plant material on the soil surface, a carbon store?
A: Litter
Q: Which biome has the largest carbon store in its vegetation?
A: Tropical rainforest
Q: Why does the tropical rainforest have high NPP?
A: High temperatures, heavy rainfall and sunlight all year allow constant growth
Q: Why does the tundra have low NPP but large soil carbon stores?
A: Cold slows growth, but it also slows decomposition so carbon builds up in soil
Q: At the continental scale, why does atmospheric CO2 fall each northern summer?
A: Northern Hemisphere land plants photosynthesise more in summer
Q: What is chemical weathering by carbonation?
A: CO2 dissolved in rain forms carbonic acid, which dissolves rocks such as limestone
Q: What weak acid forms when CO2 dissolves in rainwater?
A: Carbonic acid
Q: How does weathering of silicate rocks remove carbon from the atmosphere?
A: Carbon from CO2 is carried in solution to the sea and stored in ocean sediments
Q: How is limestone formed in the carbon cycle?
A: Shells and skeletons of marine organisms (calcium carbonate) settle and are compressed
Q: How do fossil fuels form?
A: Dead organic matter is buried and compressed without oxygen over millions of years
Q: Which process returns carbon from the lithosphere to the atmosphere naturally?
A: Volcanic outgassing
Q: What is the term for carbon carried into the mantle when oceanic plates sink?
A: Subduction
Q: Through which part of the Earth system does the fast carbon cycle mainly move carbon?
A: Living things (the biosphere), over years to centuries
Q: Through which part of the Earth system does the slow carbon cycle mainly move carbon?
A: Rocks (the lithosphere), over millions of years
Q: What is the biological pump?
A: Phytoplankton take in CO2 and their dead remains sink, moving carbon to the deep ocean
Q: What are the microscopic plants at the ocean surface that photosynthesise called?
A: Phytoplankton
Q: What is "marine snow"?
A: Dead organic particles sinking from the surface ocean to the deep sea
Q: What is the physical (solubility) pump?
A: CO2 dissolves in cold surface water, which sinks and carries carbon to the deep ocean
Q: Why does cold water absorb more CO2 than warm water?
A: Gases are more soluble in colder water
Q: What is the carbonate pump?
A: Marine organisms make calcium carbonate shells that sink and form sediments
Q: Which global ocean current, driven by temperature and salinity differences, moves dissolved carbon to the deep ocean?
A: The thermohaline circulation
Q: Where does cold, dense, salty water sink to drive the thermohaline circulation?
A: In polar seas (e.g. the North Atlantic near Greenland)
Q: What is the term for CO2 being released from the ocean to the atmosphere?
A: Outgassing
Q: What is the carbon budget?
A: The balance of carbon inputs to and outputs from the stores
Q: What does it mean when a forest has a positive carbon balance (acts as a sink)?
A: It takes up more carbon by photosynthesis than it releases
Q: How do wildfires change carbon stores?
A: They move carbon rapidly from biomass to the atmosphere as CO2
Q: How does vegetation regrowth after a wildfire balance the carbon released?
A: Regrowing plants take up CO2 by photosynthesis over years to decades
Q: Which carbon gas do volcanic eruptions release into the atmosphere?
A: Carbon dioxide (CO2)
Q: Which human activity is the largest source of extra CO2 in the atmosphere?
A: Burning fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas)
Q: Which carbon store does hydrocarbon extraction and burning take carbon from?
A: The lithosphere (fossil fuels)
Q: Apart from burning, how do fracking and gas extraction add greenhouse gases?
A: Methane leaks from wells and pipelines
Q: Which industrial process releases CO2 when limestone is heated?
A: Cement production
Q: How does deforestation change the carbon cycle?
A: It removes a carbon store and reduces photosynthesis, often releasing carbon by burning
Q: What is slash-and-burn farming?
A: Clearing forest by cutting and burning vegetation to plant crops
Q: How does ploughing release carbon?
A: It exposes soil organic matter to oxygen, speeding decomposition
Q: Which farming activity is a major source of methane from animals' digestion?
A: Cattle (livestock) farming
Q: Why are flooded rice paddies a source of methane?
A: Waterlogged soils have little oxygen, so decomposition produces methane
Q: How does urbanisation affect carbon stores?
A: Vegetation and soil are covered by concrete, reducing carbon storage
Q: How does draining peatland affect its carbon store?
A: Peat dries and oxidises, releasing CO2
Q: What is ocean acidification?
A: Falling ocean pH as the oceans absorb more CO2
Q: How does ocean acidification affect corals and shellfish?
A: It makes it harder to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons
Q: How does extra atmospheric carbon affect global climate?
A: It strengthens the greenhouse effect, raising global temperatures
Q: What is the enhanced greenhouse effect?
A: Extra warming caused by human-produced greenhouse gases

## AQA_GEOG_3.1.1.4 | content
Q: Why is the natural greenhouse effect essential for life on Earth?
A: It keeps Earth's average temperature about 15°C instead of about -18°C
Q: Which is the most abundant greenhouse gas in the atmosphere?
A: Water vapour
Q: How does the water cycle support life on land?
A: It supplies fresh water to plants, animals and people through precipitation
Q: Why is carbon essential for life on Earth?
A: It is the basis of all organic molecules (e.g. glucose, proteins, DNA)
Q: Which process links the water and carbon cycles because it needs both water and CO2?
A: Photosynthesis
Q: How does transpiration link the water and carbon cycles?
A: Plants lose water through stomata while taking in CO2 for photosynthesis
Q: How do higher atmospheric carbon levels affect the water cycle?
A: Warming increases evaporation, so the atmosphere holds more water vapour
Q: How do the water and carbon cycles combine to produce acid rain?
A: CO2 (and other gases) dissolve in rain to form weak acids
Q: How does the presence of water affect decomposition?
A: Decomposers need water, so moist soils decompose faster than dry ones
Q: Why do oceans store heat and carbon that regulate climate?
A: Water has a high heat capacity and CO2 dissolves in it
Q: In the water vapour feedback, why does warming lead to more warming?
A: Warmer air holds more water vapour, which is itself a greenhouse gas
Q: In the ice-albedo feedback, why does melting ice speed up warming?
A: Darker sea and land absorb more sunlight than reflective ice
Q: What is albedo?
A: The proportion of sunlight a surface reflects
Q: In the permafrost feedback, why does warming lead to more warming?
A: Thawing permafrost decomposes and releases CO2 and methane
Q: Why does warming of the oceans weaken the ocean carbon sink?
A: Warmer water dissolves less CO2
Q: How does forest dieback create a positive feedback on climate?
A: Dying trees store less carbon and release it, adding more CO2
Q: How do wildfires create a positive feedback on climate?
A: Warmer, drier conditions cause more fires, releasing more CO2 and causing more warming
Q: How does more low cloud act as a negative feedback on warming?
A: Low clouds reflect sunlight back to space, cooling the surface
Q: How do phytoplankton act as a negative feedback on rising CO2?
A: More CO2 and warmth boost growth, so they take up more carbon
Q: What implication of climate change on the water cycle threatens water supply in dry regions?
A: More droughts and higher evaporation reduce river flow and reservoir stores
Q: How does sea level rise threaten life on Earth?
A: Coastal flooding of low-lying land, homes and farmland
Q: How does shrinking glacier and snowpack storage affect people downstream?
A: Rivers fed by meltwater lose summer flow, reducing water supply
Q: How does ocean acidification threaten marine food chains?
A: Shell-building plankton and corals struggle, harming the species that eat them
Q: What is climate change mitigation?
A: Reducing greenhouse gas emissions or increasing carbon sinks to limit climate change
Q: What is climate change adaptation?
A: Changing how people live to cope with the effects of climate change
Q: What is carbon capture and storage (CCS)?
A: Capturing CO2 from power stations or industry and storing it underground
Q: Where is captured CO2 stored in CCS?
A: Deep in rock, e.g. depleted oil and gas fields or saline aquifers
Q: What is one weakness of CCS?
A: It is expensive and uses extra energy
Q: Why is CCS limited by geology?
A: It needs suitable deep, sealed rock formations near the emission source
Q: What is afforestation?
A: Planting trees on land that has not been forested recently
Q: What is reforestation?
A: Replanting trees on land that was recently forested
Q: How does tree planting mitigate climate change?
A: Growing trees take in CO2 by photosynthesis and store it as biomass
Q: What is one weakness of tree planting as carbon mitigation?
A: Trees take decades to grow and their carbon is lost if they burn or are felled
Q: How does no-till (zero-tillage) farming help store carbon?
A: Leaving soil unploughed reduces decomposition of soil organic matter
Q: How do cover crops help soil carbon?
A: They add organic matter and protect soil from erosion between harvests
Q: How does changing cattle feed help mitigate climate change?
A: Some feeds reduce the methane cattle produce
Q: Which change to rice growing cuts methane emissions from paddies?
A: Draining paddies at intervals (alternate wetting and drying)
Q: What is biochar?
A: Charcoal added to soil to lock up carbon for a long time
Q: How do renewable energy sources mitigate climate change?
A: They generate electricity without burning fossil fuels
Q: How does a carbon tax reduce emissions?
A: It makes polluting more expensive, encouraging cleaner choices
Q: What is an emissions trading (cap and trade) scheme?
A: A cap on total emissions with permits that firms can buy and sell
Q: What is direct air capture?
A: Machines that remove CO2 straight from the air for storage
Q: What is "debt-for-nature" swapping?
A: Cancelling part of a country's debt in return for protecting its natural environment
Q: What is one challenge of agreeing global climate action?
A: Countries disagree over who should cut emissions and pay
Q: What is an example of adaptation in farming to climate change?
A: Growing drought-resistant crops

Q: What is REDD+?
A: A UN scheme paying countries to Reduce Emissions from Deforestation and forest Degradation
Q: In which year was the Paris Agreement on climate change adopted?
A: 2015
Q: What temperature limit does the Paris Agreement aim for?
A: Well below 2°C above pre-industrial, aiming for 1.5°C
Q: In which year was the Kyoto Protocol adopted?
A: 1997
Q: Which UK law, amended in 2019, commits the UK to net zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050?
A: The Climate Change Act 2008
Q: According to the WMO, how far above the pre-industrial average was global temperature in 2024?
A: About 1.55°C (the warmest year on record)
Q: According to the IPCC AR6, how much warmer was 2011 to 2020 than 1850 to 1900?
A: About 1.1°C
Q: Which Norwegian offshore gas field has stored CO2 under the North Sea since 1996?
A: Sleipner (the world's first offshore CCS project)
Q: According to MIT, about how much CO2 does the Sleipner CCS project store each year?
A: About 0.9 million tonnes
Q: Into which rock formation is CO2 injected at Sleipner?
A: The Utsira Formation, a saline sandstone aquifer about 1 km below the seabed
Q: What 1991 policy encouraged Norway's Sleipner CCS project?
A: Norway's CO2 tax on offshore emissions
Q: In which year did the African Union launch the Great Green Wall?
A: 2007
Q: Which two countries does Africa's Great Green Wall stretch between?
A: Senegal (Atlantic coast) and Djibouti (Red Sea)
Q: According to the UNCCD, how much degraded land does the Great Green Wall aim to restore by 2030?
A: 100 million hectares
Q: According to the UNCCD, how much carbon does the Great Green Wall aim to sequester by 2030?
A: 250 million tonnes
Q: According to the UNCCD's 2020 status report, about what share of the Great Green Wall's target had been achieved?
A: About 4%
## AQA_GEOG_3.1.1.5 | content
Q: What is the principle of a simple mass balance?
A: Inputs minus outputs equals the change in storage
Q: In a water balance, what does a positive change in storage mean?
A: Inputs exceeded outputs, so the store increased
Q: A basin receives 900 mm precipitation; evapotranspiration is 400 mm and runoff 450 mm. What is the change in storage?
A: +50 mm (900 - 400 - 450)
Q: A glacier gains 3.0 m of snow (water equivalent) and loses 3.5 m by ablation in a year. What is its net mass balance?
A: -0.5 m water equivalent (it is shrinking)
Q: What is 1 cumec in litres per second?
A: 1,000 litres per second (1 m³/s)
Q: How many litres are in 1 cubic metre?
A: 1,000
Q: How many cubic metres are in 1 cubic kilometre?
A: One billion (10⁹)
Q: How many square metres are in 1 hectare?
A: 10,000
Q: How many hectares are in 1 square kilometre?
A: 100
Q: What volume of water falls as 1 mm of rain over 1 km²?
A: 1,000 m³
Q: What is 1 gigatonne (Gt) in tonnes?
A: One billion tonnes (10⁹ tonnes)
Q: What is 1 petagram (Pg) of carbon equal to in gigatonnes?
A: 1 Gt (both are 10¹⁵ grams)
Q: How do you convert a mass of carbon into the mass of CO2?
A: Multiply by 3.67 (44 ÷ 12)
Q: What does ppm mean when measuring atmospheric CO2?
A: Parts per million, the number of CO2 molecules per million air molecules
Q: A river channel has a cross-sectional area of 4 m² and a velocity of 0.5 m/s. What is its discharge?
A: 2 cumecs
Q: Peak rainfall is at 10:00 and peak discharge at 16:00. What is the lag time?
A: 6 hours
Q: On a hydrograph, which type of graph usually shows rainfall?
A: A bar graph (with discharge as a line graph)
Q: Which simple field method measures surface velocity of a stream?
A: Timing a float over a measured distance
Q: Why is a float velocity reading multiplied by about 0.8?
A: Surface flow is faster than the average flow of the whole channel
Q: Which field instrument gives a more accurate measure of stream velocity than a float?
A: A flow meter (impeller)
Q: Which field method measures how fast water soaks into the ground?
A: An infiltration ring (cylinder infiltrometer)
Q: How can interception be measured in the field?
A: Compare rain gauges under a tree canopy with gauges in the open
Q: Why is a moving average used on rainfall data?
A: It smooths short-term ups and downs to show the trend
Q: What type of diagram shows flows between stores with arrows whose width is proportional to the flow?
A: A flow (Sankey) diagram
Q: What is a weakness of measuring rainfall with only one rain gauge for a basin?
A: Rainfall varies across a basin, so one gauge may not be representative
## AQA_GEOG_3.1.1.6 | evidence
Q: Which river basin holds the largest tropical rainforest in the world?
A: The Amazon basin, South America
Q: According to NASA, what share of global river discharge to the oceans came from the Amazon in 1980 to 2009?
A: About 18%
Q: According to Staal et al. (Nature Climate Change, 2018), what share of Amazon rainfall comes from evapotranspiration within the basin?
A: About 32% (roughly one third)
Q: What are "flying rivers" in the Amazon?
A: Streams of water vapour from forest evapotranspiration carried by winds across South America
Q: According to Nobre et al. (PNAS, 2016), how much carbon does the Amazon forest store?
A: About 150 to 200 billion tonnes
Q: What is the main type of rainfall in the Amazon rainforest?
A: Convectional rainfall, often in afternoon thunderstorms
Q: Why are Amazon soils (latosols) poor in nutrients despite huge biomass?
A: Heavy rain leaches nutrients and most are held in the vegetation
Q: Why does the Amazon's litter store stay small despite large leaf fall?
A: Hot, wet conditions make decomposition very fast
Q: Which Brazilian agency's PRODES satellite system measures annual Amazon deforestation?
A: INPE (National Institute for Space Research)
Q: According to INPE PRODES, how much of the Brazilian Amazon was cleared in 2004?
A: 27,772 km²
Q: According to INPE PRODES, how much Brazilian Amazon was cleared in August 2020 to July 2021?
A: 13,235 km², the highest since 2006
Q: Under which Brazilian president did Amazon deforestation rise to a 15-year high in 2021?
A: Jair Bolsonaro
Q: According to INPE PRODES, how much Brazilian Amazon was cleared from August 2024 to July 2025?
A: 5,796 km², down 11% on the year before
Q: According to WWF, what share of Amazon deforestation in 2017 to 2022 was caused by cattle ranching?
A: About 78%
Q: According to Lovejoy and Nobre (2018), at what level of deforestation is the Amazon likely to reach a tipping point?
A: 20 to 25% of its area
Q: What do Lovejoy and Nobre predict the Amazon would turn into beyond its tipping point?
A: Savanna-like, non-forest ecosystems in the east, south and centre
Q: According to Lovejoy and Nobre (2019), what share of the whole Amazon basin had been deforested?
A: About 17%
Q: According to Gatti et al. (Nature, 2021), what has south-eastern Amazonia become?
A: A net carbon source to the atmosphere
Q: What method did Gatti et al. (2021) use to measure Amazon carbon fluxes?
A: 590 aircraft vertical profiles of CO2 and CO at four sites, 2010 to 2018
Q: In which year was the Amazon Soy Moratorium agreed?
A: 2006
Q: What did traders agree under the Amazon Soy Moratorium?
A: Not to buy soy grown on Amazon land deforested after the cut-off date
Q: In which year was the Amazon Fund set up?
A: 2008
Q: Which country has been the largest donor to Brazil's Amazon Fund?
A: Norway
Q: How much did Norway pledge to the Amazon Fund in 2008?
A: Up to US$1 billion, tied to Brazil reducing deforestation
Q: Why did Norway freeze payments to the Amazon Fund in 2019?
A: Brazil changed how the fund was governed
Q: In October 2024, what record did the Rio Negro at Manaus set?
A: Its lowest level in 122 years of records
Q: According to NPR, which ocean-atmosphere pattern, along with climate change and deforestation, worsened the 2024 Amazon drought?
A: A stronger-than-normal El Niño
Q: Which agroforestry co-operative, founded in 1989 on the Rondonia-Acre border, grows cupuacu and acai as a sustainable alternative to cattle?
A: RECA
Q: According to UNESCO, what is the largest protected area in the Amazon basin?
A: The Central Amazon Conservation Complex (a UNESCO World Heritage Site)
Q: In its 2015 Paris Agreement pledge (NDC), how much forest did Brazil commit to restore and reforest by 2030?
A: 12 million hectares
Q: Which state in the western Brazilian Amazon suffered severe drought and forest fires in 2005?
A: Acre
Q: How does Amazon deforestation reduce rainfall further downwind?
A: Less evapotranspiration means less moisture is recycled into rain
