## AQA_GEOG_3.2.1.1 | content
Q: In globalisation, what is a flow of capital?
A: The movement of money across borders (investment, loans, aid, remittances)
Q: What is the difference between foreign direct investment (FDI) and portfolio investment?
A: FDI gives lasting control of a business, portfolio investment is buying shares or bonds only
Q: In globalisation, what is a flow of labour?
A: The international migration of workers
Q: What is the term for the emigration of highly skilled and educated workers from a country?
A: Brain drain
Q: Which flow of globalisation includes banking, insurance, tourism and call centres?
A: Flows of services
Q: Which flow of globalisation includes manufactured goods and raw materials traded between countries?
A: Flows of products (trade in goods)
Q: Which flow of globalisation includes data, ideas and media sent through the internet?
A: Flows of information
Q: What is offshoring?
A: Moving part of a company's own operations to another country
Q: What is outsourcing?
A: Contracting another company to do part of the work, often in another country
Q: What is global marketing?
A: Selling a product worldwide under a single global brand and image
Q: What is the term for adapting a global product to suit local tastes and cultures?
A: Glocalisation
Q: What is a supply chain?
A: The linked steps and companies that turn raw materials into a product for the consumer
Q: What is the term for the system of supplying parts to a factory only when they are needed, cutting storage costs?
A: Just-in-time
Q: Which management system made popular by Japanese car makers aims to cut waste in production?
A: Lean production
Q: Who described the 'new international division of labour' (1980), with manufacturing moving to low-wage countries?
A: Fröbel, Heinrichs and Kreye
Q: In the new international division of labour, where do TNCs usually keep headquarters and research?
A: In high-income countries
Q: In the new international division of labour, where is routine assembly work usually located?
A: In lower-wage emerging and developing countries
Q: Traditionally, which group of countries dominated global consumption of manufactured goods?
A: High-income countries (Europe, North America, Japan)
Q: How is the global pattern of consumption changing as emerging economies grow richer?
A: Consumption is rising fast in NEEs such as China and India
Q: How did containerisation act as a factor in globalisation?
A: Standard boxes cut loading time and shipping costs, making long-distance trade cheap
Q: Why can large container ships make trade with remote places more profitable?
A: Economies of scale lower the cost per unit carried
Q: How does air freight help globalisation?
A: It moves high-value, perishable goods such as flowers or electronics quickly
Q: Which communications technology carries most international internet data between continents?
A: Undersea fibre-optic cables
Q: How have communications systems such as the internet and mobile phones driven globalisation?
A: They allow instant, cheap contact and control of operations worldwide
Q: How did deregulation of financial markets act as a factor in globalisation?
A: Capital could move freely and quickly between countries
Q: How does electronic, 24-hour trading on stock exchanges help globalisation?
A: Money moves instantly between financial centres in different time zones
Q: How do security systems, such as encrypted online payments, act as a factor in globalisation?
A: They make e-commerce and international payments safe, so trade grows
Q: How do information systems such as barcodes and tracking help globalisation?
A: TNCs track goods along global supply chains in real time
Q: How do trade agreements act as a factor in globalisation?
A: They cut tariffs and quotas, so trade between members grows
Q: What is a tariff?
A: A tax on imported goods
Q: What is a quota in international trade?
A: A limit on the amount of a good that can be imported
Q: What is the term for a barrier to trade that is not a tax, such as strict product standards?
A: A non-tariff barrier
Q: What is free trade?
A: Trade between countries with no tariffs, quotas or other barriers
Q: What is protectionism?
A: Using tariffs, quotas or subsidies to shield home industries from imports
Q: Which political and economic approach of the 1980s favoured free markets, privatisation and deregulation?
A: Neoliberalism
Q: Give an example of political globalisation.
A: Countries joining international organisations and treaties, such as the UN
Q: Give an example of social globalisation.
A: Spread of ideas and culture through migration, tourism and the internet

## AQA_GEOG_3.2.1.2 | content
Q: What is a global system?
A: A set of interconnected parts (states, firms, flows) working at a global scale
Q: What is interdependence?
A: When countries rely on each other, so a change in one affects others
Q: What is economic interdependence?
A: Countries relying on each other for trade, investment, jobs and supply chains
Q: What is political interdependence?
A: Countries linked through alliances, treaties and international organisations
Q: What is social interdependence?
A: Links between people in different countries through migration, culture and family ties
Q: What is environmental interdependence?
A: Environmental change in one place has consequences for environments elsewhere
Q: Why are all countries environmentally interdependent?
A: They share global commons such as the atmosphere and oceans
Q: How can flows of FDI promote growth in a host country?
A: They bring jobs, skills, technology and tax revenue
Q: How can flows of remittances promote development?
A: Families spend them on food, housing, education and healthcare
Q: How can unequal flows of money cause inequality within a country?
A: Investment concentrates in a few cities or regions, widening the gap with rural areas
Q: In global systems, what is an injustice?
A: Unfair treatment of people or places, such as exploitative pay and conditions
Q: How can global demand for resources cause conflict?
A: Groups or states fight over control of valuable resources such as oil or minerals
Q: Which sociologist developed world systems theory (1974)?
A: Immanuel Wallerstein
Q: In Wallerstein's world systems theory, what is the core?
A: Rich states with high-skill, high-wage, high-tech production that dominate the system
Q: In Wallerstein's world systems theory, what is the periphery?
A: Poor states supplying raw materials and cheap labour to the core
Q: In Wallerstein's world systems theory, what is the semi-periphery?
A: States with features of both core and periphery, such as emerging economies
Q: According to Wallerstein, how does the core benefit from the periphery?
A: Unequal exchange transfers wealth from periphery to core
Q: Which economist argued in the 1960s that rich countries cause the 'development of underdevelopment' in poor countries?
A: Andre Gunder Frank
Q: What does dependency theory argue?
A: Poor countries stay poor because they depend on and are exploited by rich ones
Q: What is neo-colonialism?
A: Control of poorer countries by richer states or TNCs through economic power, not direct rule
Q: What is hegemony?
A: Dominance of one state over others through economic, military or cultural power
Q: What is a superpower?
A: A state able to project its power and influence anywhere in the world
Q: What is geopolitics?
A: How geography, power and politics combine to shape relations between states
Q: Who coined the term 'soft power'?
A: Joseph Nye
Q: What is soft power?
A: Influence through attraction, such as culture and values, rather than force
Q: What is hard power?
A: Influence through military force or economic pressure, such as sanctions
Q: How do powerful states drive global systems to their own advantage?
A: They set rules in bodies like the IMF, WTO and UN Security Council
Q: How can smaller, weaker states resist powerful states in global systems?
A: By forming blocs or alliances to bargain together
Q: Why are countries that depend on one primary export vulnerable?
A: A fall in its world price cuts export earnings sharply
Q: What do terms of trade measure?
A: The price of a country's exports relative to the price of its imports
Q: What does the Prebisch-Singer hypothesis argue about primary product exporters?
A: Their terms of trade tend to worsen over time against manufactured goods
Q: What is the digital divide?
A: The gap between people with and without access to the internet and digital technology
Q: Why can a financial crisis in one country spread worldwide?
A: Banks and markets are interdependent, so losses pass between them

## AQA_GEOG_3.2.1.3 | content
Q: Since 1950, how has the growth of world trade compared with the growth of world output (GDP)?
A: Trade has grown faster than output
Q: How has the share of world trade between developing countries (south-south trade) changed in recent decades?
A: It has grown rapidly
Q: What is intra-firm trade?
A: Trade between branches of the same TNC in different countries
Q: What is a trading bloc?
A: A group of countries that agree to reduce or remove trade barriers between members
Q: What is a free trade area?
A: Members remove tariffs between themselves but keep their own tariffs on others
Q: What does a customs union add to a free trade area?
A: A common external tariff on imports from non-members
Q: What does a common (single) market add to a customs union?
A: Free movement of labour, capital and services
Q: What is a bilateral trade agreement?
A: A trade deal between two countries
Q: What is a multilateral trade agreement?
A: A trade deal among many countries, such as through the WTO
Q: What is trade diversion?
A: Trade shifting from a cheaper outside supplier to a member of the same bloc
Q: What is a trade surplus?
A: When the value of a country's exports is greater than its imports
Q: What is a trade deficit?
A: When the value of a country's imports is greater than its exports
Q: What is the term for higher tariffs on processed goods than on raw materials?
A: Tariff escalation
Q: How does tariff escalation harm developing countries?
A: It discourages them from processing raw materials and adding value
Q: What is added value?
A: The increase in worth of a product at each stage of processing
Q: How do farm subsidies in rich countries harm farmers in poorer countries?
A: Subsidised crops are sold cheaply on world markets, undercutting them
Q: What is dumping in international trade?
A: Selling exports abroad below the cost of production
Q: What is preferential access in trade?
A: Lower or no tariffs for goods from particular countries, often the poorest
Q: What is the WTO term for special rights that give developing countries more time and better market access?
A: Special and Differential Treatment
Q: How can differential access to markets reduce a country's economic well-being?
A: Exports face high barriers, so jobs and income from trade are lost
Q: How can access to a large market improve a country's societal well-being?
A: Trade income funds better health and education services
Q: Why do landlocked countries often have weaker access to world markets?
A: They pay more to move goods through other countries to ports
Q: Why can membership of a trade bloc be a risk for a smaller economy?
A: It becomes dependent on bigger members, so their recessions hit it hard
Q: What does Fairtrade guarantee producers?
A: A minimum price plus a social premium for community projects
Q: What is a transnational corporation (TNC)?
A: A company that operates in more than one country
Q: Where are a TNC's headquarters and research usually located?
A: In its home country, often near universities
Q: Why do TNCs locate manufacturing in emerging economies?
A: Lower labour costs and access to growing markets
Q: What is vertical integration?
A: A TNC owning different stages of the same supply chain
Q: What is horizontal integration?
A: A TNC buying or merging with firms at the same stage, often rivals
Q: What is a backward linkage for a TNC factory?
A: Local firms supplying parts or services to the factory
Q: What is subcontracting?
A: A TNC paying another firm to make products or parts for it
Q: Why do TNCs use subcontractors in low-wage countries?
A: To cut costs and avoid responsibility for running factories
Q: What is transfer pricing by a TNC?
A: Setting prices between its own branches to move profit to low-tax countries
Q: What does it mean that a TNC is 'footloose'?
A: It is not tied to one location and can relocate easily
Q: What is an Export Processing Zone (EPZ)?
A: An area offering tax breaks and few regulations to attract export industries
Q: What is the 'race to the bottom'?
A: Countries cutting wages, taxes or regulations to compete for TNC investment
Q: What is profit leakage?
A: Profits made in a host country flowing back to the TNC's home country
Q: Which positive impact of TNCs comes from training local workers?
A: Transfer of skills and technology
Q: What negative impact can a TNC have on its home country when it moves production abroad?
A: Factory closures and job losses (deindustrialisation)
Q: How do TNCs use global marketing?
A: One brand and advertising campaign promoted worldwide, adapted locally
Q: What is a global commodity chain?
A: The linked stages a commodity goes through from producer to consumer
Q: In a food commodity chain, which group usually receives the smallest share of the final price?
A: The primary producers (farmers)

A: The rules, norms and institutions that regulate global issues without a world government

A: A state's right to govern its own territory without outside interference

A: States can refuse to sign or obey international agreements
Q: What does it mean for a state to ratify a treaty?

Q: What is an intergovernmental organisation (IGO)?
A: A body whose members are national governments, such as the UN
Q: What is a non-governmental organisation (NGO)?
A: A non-profit body independent of government, such as Greenpeace
Q: What does it mean that global institutions 'reproduce' global systems?
A: Their rules keep the existing balance of power in place
Q: Why do critics say the IMF and World Bank favour rich countries?
A: Voting power depends on money paid in, so rich countries dominate
Q: What are IMF loan 'conditions' called?
A: Conditionality

Q: Which economist named the 'Washington Consensus' of free-market policies in 1989?

Q: What is the main role of the IMF?

Q: What is the main role of the World Bank?

Q: What is the main role of the WTO?
A: To set trade rules, cut barriers and settle trade disputes between members

Q: Why can the WTO's dispute settlement process exacerbate inequality?

Q: Why is the UN Security Council veto criticised?

Q: What is UNEP's role in global governance?
A: It coordinates UN environmental work and helps create environmental treaties
Q: How can global agencies promote stability?
A: Through emergency loans, peacekeeping and agreed rules for trade
