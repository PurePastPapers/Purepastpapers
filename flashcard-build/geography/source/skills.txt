Q: How many days of fieldwork must AQA A-level Geography students undertake during the course?
A: Four days
Q: Fieldwork for AQA A-level Geography must relate to processes in which two branches of geography?
A: Physical and human geography
Q: Must the four days of A-level fieldwork be full days taken away from the local area?
A: No, they are local or further afield, on full or part days
Q: By what date must a school send AQA the fieldwork statement confirming students' four days of fieldwork?
A: 15 May in the year of entry
Q: Under AQA rules, how is a late or missing fieldwork statement treated?
A: As malpractice or maladministration
Q: What is the advisory word count for the AQA A-level Geography NEA independent investigation?
A: 3,000 to 4,000 words
Q: How many marks is the AQA A-level Geography NEA (Component 3) worth?
A: 60 marks
Q: What percentage of the AQA A-level Geography qualification is the NEA investigation?
A: 20%
Q: Who marks the AQA A-level Geography NEA investigation?
A: Teachers, with moderation by AQA
Q: What does AQA call the A-level Geography NEA, which must include data collected in the field?
A: The independent investigation (geography fieldwork investigation)
Q: Who must define and develop the question or issue for an A-level Geography NEA investigation?
A: The student, individually
Q: Can an NEA investigation focus on human geography only, physical only, or a combination?
A: Any of these: human, physical or a combination of both
Q: Which part of the A-level Geography specification can an NEA investigation relate to?
A: Any part of the specification content
Q: Does the NEA word count include photographs and data presentation such as graphs?
A: Yes, it includes photographs and data presentation (and all text and text boxes)
Q: Do appendices count towards the NEA word count?
A: No
Q: What should NEA appendices contain, according to AQA?
A: Examples of raw data only, such as one data sheet or questionnaire
Q: According to AQA, what is the risk of writing an NEA report below the advisory word count?
A: Not covering the required assessment objectives fully
Q: According to AQA, what is the risk of writing an NEA report above the advisory word count?
A: A lack of precision and focus
Q: What is the title of NEA mark scheme Area 1?
A: Introduction and preliminary research
Q: How many marks is NEA Area 1 (Introduction and preliminary research) worth?
A: 10 marks
Q: What is the title of NEA mark scheme Area 2?
A: Methods of field investigation
Q: How many marks is NEA Area 2 (Methods of field investigation) worth?
A: 15 marks
Q: What is the title of NEA mark scheme Area 3?
A: Methods of critical analysis
Q: How many marks is NEA Area 3 (Methods of critical analysis) worth?
A: 20 marks
Q: What is the title of NEA mark scheme Area 4?
A: Conclusions, evaluation and presentation
Q: How many marks is NEA Area 4 (Conclusions, evaluation and presentation) worth?
A: 15 marks
Q: Which assessment objective carries all 10 marks of NEA Area 1?
A: AO3
Q: How many of the 20 marks in NEA Area 3 (critical analysis) are AO2?
A: 6 marks (the other 14 are AO3)
Q: How many levels does the NEA mark scheme use for each area?
A: Four levels (plus no marks)
Q: In NEA Area 1, what must the research question be linked to?
A: The specification
Q: In NEA Area 1, what must literature sources be used to set out for the research question?
A: The theoretical or comparative context
Q: Which form must accompany each student's NEA, signed by student and teacher to confirm authenticity?
A: The Candidate record form (CRF)
Q: Which section of the Candidate record form must the student complete at the planning stage of the NEA?
A: The independent investigation proposal
Q: In the NEA, at which stage is collaboration in a group allowed besides exploring possible topics?
A: Collecting field data
Q: Must NEA students analyse, present and draw conclusions from their data independently?
A: Yes, these stages must be done independently
Q: Can secondary data used in an NEA be supplied by the teacher rather than sourced by the student?
A: No, secondary data must be sourced by the student
Q: What must a teacher do if a student gets help beyond general advice on their NEA?
A: Record it on the CRF and take it into account when marking
Q: Are teachers allowed to give NEA students a list of titles to choose from?
A: No
Q: Are teachers allowed to mark an NEA draft and share the mark so the student can improve it?
A: No
Q: According to AQA, which dimension of field research must the NEA evaluation show an understanding of?
A: The ethical dimensions of field research
Q: According to AQA, besides a summary of results, what must the NEA conclusion include?
A: The relevance of results to the title and an evaluation of the investigation
Q: What is a hypothesis in a geographical investigation?
A: A testable statement predicting a relationship or difference
Q: In the fieldwork enquiry process, which stage comes after choosing a question or hypothesis?
A: Planning and collecting data
Q: In the fieldwork enquiry process, which stage follows data presentation?
A: Analysis and interpretation of the data
Q: What is the final stage of the fieldwork enquiry process?
A: Evaluation of the investigation
Q: What is the purpose of a literature review at the start of an investigation?
A: To set the question in its theoretical and comparative context
Q: What is a pilot study in fieldwork?
A: A small trial run to test methods before the main data collection
Q: What is primary data?
A: Data collected first-hand by the investigator
Q: What is secondary data?
A: Data collected by someone else, such as census data or published reports
Q: Give one strength of primary data for an investigation.
A: The investigator controls how, where and when it is collected
Q: Give one strength of secondary data for an investigation.
A: It covers larger areas or longer time periods than a student could collect
Q: What is quantitative data?
A: Numerical data that can be measured or counted
Q: What is qualitative data?
A: Non-numerical data such as opinions, descriptions, photos or interview text
Q: Give one strength of quantitative data.
A: It can be tested statistically
Q: Give one strength of qualitative data.
A: It gives depth, such as people's feelings and meanings
Q: What is a population in sampling terms?
A: The whole group or area that the investigation is about
Q: Why do geographers sample rather than measure everything?
A: It saves time and money when measuring everything is impossible
Q: What is random sampling?
A: Every member or point has an equal chance of being chosen
Q: How are sample points chosen in random sampling?
A: Using random number tables or a random number generator
Q: Give one strength of random sampling.
A: It avoids investigator bias
Q: Give one limitation of random sampling.
A: Parts of the area are often missed or under-represented by chance
Q: What is systematic sampling?
A: Choosing samples at regular intervals, such as every 10 m or every 5th person
Q: Give one strength of systematic sampling.
A: It is quick and gives even coverage of the area
Q: Give one limitation of systematic sampling.
A: It is biased if a pattern in the area matches the sampling interval
Q: What is stratified sampling?
A: Sampling each subgroup in proportion to its share of the population
Q: Give one strength of stratified sampling.
A: Every subgroup is represented in its correct proportion
Q: Give one limitation of stratified sampling.
A: You must know the size of each subgroup in advance
Q: A population is 60% female and 40% male. How many males should a stratified sample of 50 include?
A: 20
Q: What is opportunistic (convenience) sampling?
A: Sampling whoever or whatever is easiest to reach at the time
Q: Give one limitation of opportunistic sampling.
A: It is biased and unlikely to be representative
Q: What is point sampling?
A: Taking measurements at chosen individual points
Q: What is line sampling?
A: Taking measurements along a line, such as a transect
Q: What is area sampling?
A: Taking measurements within a defined area, such as a quadrat
Q: What is a transect in fieldwork?
A: A line across an area along which measurements are taken
Q: What is a quadrat?
A: A square frame used to sample an area, such as for vegetation cover
Q: What is a risk assessment in fieldwork?
A: Identifying hazards, how likely they are and how to reduce them
Q: In a risk assessment, what is a hazard?
A: Something that could cause harm, such as tides or traffic
Q: What is informed consent in fieldwork with people?
A: Participants agree after being told the purpose and use of the research
Q: What is anonymity in fieldwork with people?
A: Participants' names and identities are not recorded or revealed
Q: What is confidentiality in fieldwork with people?
A: Information given is kept private and only used as agreed
Q: What is the right to withdraw in fieldwork with people?
A: Participants can stop taking part and have their data removed
## AQA_GEOG_3.4.1 | content
Q: What makes data geographical?
A: It is linked to a location (spatially referenced)
Q: What is coding when analysing qualitative data?
A: Sorting text or images into categories or themes so they can be analysed
Q: Why does coding qualitative data actively shape a geographical representation?
A: The researcher chooses the categories, so their choices shape the result
Q: Give one limitation of coding qualitative data.
A: It is subjective, so different researchers code the same text differently
Q: What is a structured interview?
A: An interview with fixed questions asked in the same order to everyone
Q: What is a semi-structured interview?
A: An interview with set questions plus freedom to follow up answers
Q: Give one strength of a semi-structured interview over a questionnaire.
A: It allows follow-up questions that give more depth
Q: What is an open question?
A: A question that lets the respondent answer in their own words
Q: What is a closed question?
A: A question with fixed answer options, such as yes/no or tick boxes
Q: Give one strength of closed questions.
A: Answers are easy to count and compare
Q: Give one limitation of closed questions.
A: They limit answers to the options given, so detail is lost
Q: What is a Likert scale?
A: A scale where respondents rate agreement, such as from 1 to 5
Q: What is a bipolar survey, such as an environmental quality survey?
A: Scoring a site between two opposite words, such as -3 (ugly) to +3 (attractive)
Q: Give one limitation of a bipolar environmental quality survey.
A: Scores are subjective and differ between observers
Q: What is a socio-political implication of representing data about a community?
A: Negative representations can stigmatise the community
Q: What is the difference between accuracy and precision in measurement?
A: Accuracy is closeness to the true value, precision is how close repeat readings are
Q: What is reliability in fieldwork data?
A: Getting the same results if the method is repeated
Q: What is a systematic error?
A: An error that shifts every reading the same way, such as a badly zeroed scale
Q: What is a random error?
A: An unpredictable error that varies from reading to reading
Q: What is an anomaly (outlier) in a data set?
A: A value that does not fit the overall pattern
Q: What do descriptive statistics do?
A: Summarise a data set, such as its average and spread
Q: What do inferential statistics do?
A: Test whether a result is significant or likely due to chance
Q: What is a measure of central tendency?
A: A single value representing the middle or typical value, such as the mean
Q: What is a measure of dispersion?
A: A measure of how spread out the data are, such as the range
Q: What is a relational statistic?
A: A test of the relationship between two variables, such as Spearman's rank
Q: What is nominal data?
A: Data in named categories with no order, such as land use type
Q: What is ordinal data?
A: Data that can be put in rank order
Q: What is interval or ratio data?
A: Measured numerical data on a scale with equal intervals

## AQA_GEOG_3.4.2.2 | content
Q: What is a choropleth map?
A: A map with areas shaded by value, darker shades for higher values
Q: What kind of data should be used for a choropleth map: raw totals or values per area?
A: Values per area, such as densities or percentages
Q: Give one limitation of a choropleth map.
A: It suggests a uniform value across each area and sudden changes at boundaries
Q: What is an isoline map?
A: A map with lines joining places of equal value
Q: What are isolines of equal pressure called?
A: Isobars
Q: What are isolines of equal temperature called?
A: Isotherms
Q: What kind of data does an isoline map need?
A: Continuous data that changes gradually across space
Q: Give one limitation of an isoline map.
A: Lines are estimated between data points, so they are inaccurate where points are few
Q: What is a dot map?
A: A map where each dot represents a fixed quantity
Q: Give one strength of a dot map.
A: It shows distribution and density clearly
Q: Give one limitation of a dot map.
A: Dots merge in dense areas so they are hard to count
Q: What is a flow-line map?
A: A map with lines along routes, their width proportional to the amount of movement
Q: What is a desire-line map?
A: Straight lines from origin to destination showing movement, not the actual route
Q: What does each line show on a trip-line map?
A: One individual journey from origin to destination
Q: What is a proportional symbol map?
A: A map with symbols whose size is proportional to the value at each place
Q: For proportional circles, what is made proportional to the value?
A: The area of the circle
Q: Give one limitation of proportional symbols.
A: Large symbols overlap and hide each other or the map
Q: What is a located graph?
A: A graph placed on a map at the location its data refer to
Q: What is a synoptic chart?
A: A weather map showing pressure, fronts and weather at one time
Q: On a synoptic chart, what do closely spaced isobars show?
A: Strong winds
Q: On a synoptic chart, what is shown by isobars around low pressure?
A: A depression
Q: On a synoptic chart, what is shown by isobars around high pressure?
A: An anticyclone
Q: What is an atlas map?
A: A small-scale map showing patterns over large areas, such as countries
## AQA_GEOG_3.4.2.3 | content
Q: Which graph type shows continuous change over time?
A: A line graph
Q: What is a compound line graph?
A: Lines stacked so each band shows one part and the top line shows the total
Q: What is a comparative bar graph?
A: Bars for two or more data sets drawn side by side to compare
Q: What is a compound (divided) bar graph?
A: A bar split into parts that make up the total
Q: What is a divergent bar graph?
A: Bars drawn above and below (or either side of) a central zero line
Q: When is a divergent graph used?
A: For data with positive and negative values, such as bipolar scores
Q: What does a scatter graph show?
A: The relationship between two variables
Q: On a scatter graph, which axis is the independent variable plotted on?
A: The x (horizontal) axis
Q: What does a best fit line on a scatter graph show?
A: The trend of the relationship
Q: Through which point should a best fit line pass?
A: The mean point (mean of x, mean of y)
Q: What does a best fit line rising from left to right show?
A: A positive correlation
Q: What does a best fit line falling from left to right show?
A: A negative correlation
Q: Does a strong correlation on a scatter graph prove that one variable causes the other?
A: No, correlation does not prove causation
Q: When is a logarithmic scale used on a graph?
A: When data cover a very large range of values
Q: On a logarithmic scale, what happens to values with each cycle?
A: They increase tenfold (x10)
Q: What is a semi-logarithmic graph?
A: A graph with one log axis and one normal (arithmetic) axis
Q: Which value is impossible to plot on a logarithmic scale?
A: Zero
Q: What is a triangular graph used for?
A: Three variables that add up to 100%
Q: Which classic data set is plotted on a triangular graph in physical geography?
A: Soil or sediment texture (sand, silt and clay)
Q: How many degrees of a pie chart represent 25% of the total?
A: 90 degrees
Q: How do you convert a percentage to degrees for a pie chart?
A: Multiply by 3.6
Q: What is a proportional divided circle?
A: A pie chart whose circle area is proportional to the total
Q: For proportional circles, the circle radius is proportional to what?
A: The square root of the value
Q: One proportional circle shows 400, another 100. How many times larger is the first circle's radius?
A: 2 times (square root of 4)
Q: What is a dispersion diagram?
A: Individual values plotted as dots along a vertical scale to show spread
Q: Which statistics are read easily from a dispersion diagram?
A: The median and interquartile range
Q: What does a kite diagram show?
A: Abundance or cover of species along a transect
Q: Where is a kite diagram commonly used in physical geography fieldwork?
A: Vegetation succession across sand dunes
Q: What does a rose diagram show?
A: Direction and frequency, such as wind direction
Q: Besides wind direction, name one use of a rose diagram in geography.
A: Pebble (clast) orientation (also: corrie aspect)
Q: What does a Lorenz curve plot?
A: Cumulative % of a variable against cumulative % of the population
Q: What does the straight diagonal line on a Lorenz curve graph represent?
A: Perfect equality
Q: On a Lorenz curve, what does a curve far from the diagonal show?
A: High inequality (uneven distribution)
Q: What does a box and whisker plot show?
A: Median, quartiles and range of a data set
## AQA_GEOG_3.4.2.4 | content
Q: How is the mean calculated?
A: Add all the values and divide by the number of values
Q: What is the median?
A: The middle value when data are in rank order
Q: What is the mode?
A: The most frequent value
Q: Why is the median often better than the mean for data with extreme values?
A: The median is not affected by outliers
Q: Which measure of central tendency suits nominal (category) data?
A: The mode
Q: Worked example: what is the mean of 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11?
A: 7
Q: Worked example: what is the median of 2, 4, 6 and 8?
A: 5
Q: Worked example: what is the mode of 4, 6, 6, 6, 9 and 9?
A: 6
Q: What is the range?
A: The highest value minus the lowest value
Q: Give one limitation of the range as a measure of dispersion.
A: It is distorted by one extreme value
Q: Worked example: what is the range of 12, 3, 27 and 8?
A: 24
Q: What is the interquartile range?
A: The upper quartile minus the lower quartile
Q: What part of a data set does the interquartile range describe?
A: The spread of the middle 50% of values
Q: Give one strength of the interquartile range over the range.
A: It is not affected by extreme values
Q: Worked example: what is the interquartile range of 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 and 13?
A: 8 (upper quartile 11 minus lower quartile 3)
Q: What does standard deviation measure?
A: The average spread of values around the mean
Q: What does a small standard deviation show?
A: Values are clustered closely around the mean
Q: What does a large standard deviation show?
A: Values are widely spread from the mean
Q: In the standard deviation formula used on AQA papers, what is divided by n before taking the square root?
A: The sum of (x minus mean) squared
Q: In a normal distribution, roughly what percentage of values lie within 1 standard deviation of the mean?
A: About 68%
Q: Worked example: what is the standard deviation (dividing by n) of 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7 and 9?
A: 2 (mean 5, sum of squared deviations 32, 32/8 = 4, square root 2)
Q: What does Spearman's rank correlation test?
A: The strength and direction of the relationship between two variables
Q: What type of data does Spearman's rank use?
A: Paired data converted to ranks
Q: What is the formula for Spearman's rank correlation coefficient?
A: rs = 1 - (6 x sum of d squared) / (n cubed - n)
Q: In the Spearman's rank formula, what is d?
A: The difference between the two ranks for each pair
Q: In the Spearman's rank formula, what is n?
A: The number of pairs of data
Q: Between which values does Spearman's rank coefficient always lie?
A: -1 and +1
Q: What does a Spearman's rank value of +1 mean?
A: Perfect positive correlation
Q: What does a Spearman's rank value of -1 mean?
A: Perfect negative correlation
Q: What does a Spearman's rank value close to 0 mean?
A: No correlation
Q: Worked example: for 5 pairs with sum of d squared = 4, what is Spearman's rs?
A: 0.8 (1 - 24/120)
Q: Worked example: for 5 pairs with sum of d squared = 40, what is Spearman's rs?
A: -1 (1 - 240/120)
Q: When ranking, how are tied values ranked?
A: Each gets the average of the ranks they would share
Q: Worked example: ranking 10, 8, 8 and 5 from highest, what rank do the two 8s get?
A: 2.5 each
Q: How do you test whether a Spearman's rank result is significant?
A: Compare rs with the critical value for n in a significance table
Q: When is a Spearman's rank result significant?
A: When rs (ignoring its sign) is equal to or above the critical value
Q: What does the chi-squared test assess?
A: Whether observed frequencies differ significantly from expected frequencies
Q: What is the formula for chi-squared?
A: Sum of (O - E) squared / E
Q: In the chi-squared formula, what are O and E?
A: Observed and expected frequencies
Q: What type of data does the chi-squared test need?
A: Frequencies (counts) in categories, not percentages
Q: In a chi-squared test, what is the minimum expected frequency usually required in each category?
A: 5
Q: How are degrees of freedom found for a chi-squared contingency table?
A: (rows - 1) x (columns - 1)
Q: How are degrees of freedom found for a one-row chi-squared test with k categories?
A: k - 1
Q: Worked example: how many degrees of freedom does a 3 row by 4 column chi-squared table have?
A: 6
Q: Worked example: observed counts 30 and 10, expected 20 and 20. What is chi-squared?
A: 10 (100/20 + 100/20)
Q: What is the critical value of chi-squared at 1 degree of freedom and 0.05 significance?
A: 3.84
Q: When is a chi-squared result significant?
A: When it is equal to or above the critical value
Q: What does the Mann-Whitney U test assess?
A: Whether two independent samples differ significantly
Q: What is the formula for U1 in the Mann-Whitney U test?
A: U1 = n1 x n2 + n1(n1 + 1)/2 - R1
Q: In the Mann-Whitney U formula, what is R1?
A: The sum of the ranks of sample 1
Q: In the Mann-Whitney U test, which U value is compared with the critical value?
A: The smaller of U1 and U2
Q: When is a Mann-Whitney U result significant?
A: When the smaller U is equal to or below the critical value
Q: Worked example: two samples of 5, sample 1 rank sum R1 = 20. What is U1?
A: 20 (25 + 15 - 20)
Q: What does nearest neighbour analysis measure?
A: Whether a point pattern is clustered, random or regular
Q: What is the formula for the nearest neighbour index (Rn)?
A: Rn = 2 x mean distance x square root of (n / area)
Q: What nearest neighbour index value shows a perfectly clustered pattern?
A: 0
Q: What nearest neighbour index value shows a random pattern?
A: 1.0
Q: What nearest neighbour index value shows a perfectly regular (uniform) pattern?
A: 2.15
Q: Worked example: mean nearest neighbour distance 1 km, 25 points, area 100 km squared. What is Rn?
A: 1.0, a random pattern
Q: Worked example: mean nearest neighbour distance 0.1 km, 25 points, area 100 km squared. What is Rn?
A: 0.1, a clustered pattern
Q: What does the Gini coefficient measure?
A: Inequality in a distribution, such as income
Q: What Gini coefficient value means perfect equality?
A: 0
Q: What Gini coefficient value means perfect inequality?
A: 1 (or 100 when written as a percentage)
Q: From which graph is the Gini coefficient derived?
A: The Lorenz curve
Q: What is a null hypothesis?
A: A statement that there is no significant relationship or difference
Q: What is an alternative hypothesis?
A: A statement that there is a significant relationship or difference
Q: What happens to the null hypothesis when a test result is significant?
A: It is rejected
Q: What does a significance level of 0.05 mean?
A: A 5% probability the result occurred by chance (95% confidence)
Q: What does a significance level of 0.01 mean?
A: A 1% probability the result occurred by chance (99% confidence)
Q: What is the minimum significance level usually accepted in geography?
A: 0.05 (95% confidence)
Q: What are degrees of freedom in a statistical test?
A: The number of values free to vary, used to choose the critical value
Q: What is a critical value?
A: The value from a table that a test result must reach to be significant

Q: What is crowd sourcing of data?
A: Collecting data from large numbers of the public, often online
Q: Give one limitation of crowd-sourced data.
A: Its accuracy is hard to check
Q: What is big data?
A: Extremely large data sets, such as phone location data, analysed by computer
Q: What is an electronic database?
A: An organised store of data held on a computer that can be searched
Q: What is GPS used for in fieldwork?
A: Recording the exact location of data points
Q: What is the difference between raster and vector data in GIS?
A: Raster is a grid of cells, vector uses points, lines and polygons
Q: Give one strength of using ICT to produce graphs and statistics.
A: It is fast and reduces calculation errors
