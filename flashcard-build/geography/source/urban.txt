## AQA_GEOG_3.2.3.1 | content
Q: What is the term for the increase in the proportion of a country's population living in urban areas?
A: Urbanisation
Q: How does urban growth differ from urbanisation?
A: Urban growth is an increase in the number of urban residents, not their share of the population
Q: Besides rural-urban migration, which process adds to urban population growth?
A: Natural increase (more births than deaths)
Q: Why does rural-urban migration also raise urban natural increase?
A: Migrants are mostly young adults of child-bearing age
Q: In urbanisation, what is a push factor?
A: A negative feature of the source area that drives people away, e.g. rural poverty or drought
Q: In urbanisation, what is a pull factor?
A: A perceived attraction of the destination, e.g. jobs, higher wages or better services
Q: Since 1945, in which group of countries has most urbanisation taken place?
A: Lower-income and emerging economies, especially in Asia and Africa
Q: Why did urbanisation in most HICs slow down after about 1950?
A: Most of their population was already urban, so the urban share neared its ceiling
Q: What is the term for the outward movement of people and activities from inner cities to the edges of the built-up area?
A: Suburbanisation
Q: Which transport change in the 20th century most enabled suburbanisation in HICs?
A: Mass car ownership (earlier also trams and commuter railways)
Q: Which American geographer coined the term counter-urbanisation in 1976?
A: Brian Berry
Q: Which technological change since the 1990s has encouraged counter-urbanisation by freeing people from daily commuting?
A: The internet and broadband, allowing home working
Q: What is urban resurgence (re-urbanisation)?
A: Population and investment returning to inner city areas after a period of decline
Q: Which groups typically lead urban resurgence in inner city areas?
A: Young professionals and students attracted by city-centre jobs and leisure
Q: What is studentification?
A: The concentration of students in a neighbourhood, changing its housing and services
Q: What population size defines a megacity?
A: More than 10 million people
Q: What is a world city (global city)?
A: A city with a disproportionate influence over the global economy, politics and culture
Q: Which geographer used the term 'world cities' in a 1966 book of that name?
A: Peter Hall
Q: Which sociologist wrote 'The Global City' (1991), about London, New York and Tokyo?
A: Saskia Sassen
Q: Which research network ranks world cities by their links through advanced producer services such as banking and law?
A: GaWC (Globalization and World Cities Research Network)
Q: Why do world cities have a role far beyond their own country?
A: They are hubs of TNC headquarters, finance and global flows of capital, people and ideas
Q: Why is a megacity not necessarily a world city?
A: Population size alone does not give global economic or political influence
Q: Which economic process drove 19th century urbanisation in Britain?
A: Industrialisation, with factory jobs concentrated in towns
Q: Which demographic process mainly drives urban growth in today's lower-income countries?
A: Rural-urban migration combined with high natural increase
Q: How do social processes such as better healthcare raise urban growth?
A: Lower death rates in cities increase natural increase
Q: Which type of process is a government restricting or relaxing internal migration, such as China's hukou system?
A: A political process
Q: What is China's hukou system?
A: A household registration system that limited rural migrants' rights to urban services
Q: What is the global shift?
A: The movement of manufacturing from HICs to lower-cost countries, mainly in Asia
Q: Which effect of deindustrialisation leaves inner city sites empty and polluted?
A: Dereliction of old factories, docks and warehouses
Q: What is urban decentralisation?
A: The outward relocation of people, shops, offices or industry from the city centre to the edge
Q: Which pull factor attracts retailers and businesses to edge-of-city sites?
A: Large areas of cheaper land with good road access and parking
Q: Which push factor encourages businesses to leave the CBD?
A: High rents and land prices, plus congestion and lack of space to expand
Q: Which economic sector grows as deindustrialisation takes place in HIC cities?
A: The service economy (tertiary and quaternary sectors)
Q: What is the quaternary sector?
A: Knowledge-based work such as research, IT and finance consultancy
Q: Which recent change in working patterns has reduced demand for city-centre offices?
A: The growth of home working after the COVID-19 pandemic
Q: What is meant by property-led regeneration?
A: Regeneration driven by private developers building offices and housing to attract investment
Q: What is the main criticism of top-down, property-led regeneration?
A: Benefits bypass local residents, who are often priced out
Q: What is the trickle-down idea behind 1980s UK urban policy?
A: Private investment would create wealth that would spread to local people
Q: What is the main feature of partnership-based regeneration since the 1990s?
A: Public, private and community groups work together, often bidding for funding
Q: What is an Enterprise Zone?
A: An area with tax breaks and simplified planning rules to attract business investment
Q: What is an Urban Development Corporation (UDC)?
A: A government-appointed body that took over planning powers to regenerate a run-down area
Q: Why were UDCs criticised as undemocratic?
A: They took planning powers away from elected local councils
Q: What is a brownfield site?
A: Previously developed land, often derelict, available for re-use
Q: What is a greenfield site?
A: Land that has not been built on before, usually farmland at the urban edge

## AQA_GEOG_3.2.3.2 | content
Q: What is meant by urban form?
A: The physical shape, size, density and layout of a settlement
Q: Which physical factor explains why many cities grew at a bridging point or confluence of rivers?
A: Site and water supply (a wet point or crossing point)
Q: How does steep relief affect urban form?
A: It limits building, giving lower densities or growth along valleys
Q: How does a coastline or estuary affect urban form?
A: It blocks growth on one side, giving a linear or semicircular shape
Q: Which human factor gives cities a star-shaped or ribbon form?
A: Growth along main roads and railway lines
Q: Which planning policy around UK cities stops outward urban sprawl?
A: Green belts
Q: What does CBD stand for?
A: Central Business District
Q: Why are land values highest in the CBD?
A: It is the most accessible point, so competition for land is greatest
Q: What is the bid-rent theory?
A: Land users bid most for accessible central sites, so land use changes with distance from the centre
Q: Which model of urban land use (1925) shows concentric rings around the CBD?
A: The Burgess model
Q: Which model (1939) shows land use in sectors along transport routes?
A: The Hoyt model
Q: Which model (1945) shows a city with several centres of growth?
A: The multiple nuclei model (Harris and Ullman)
Q: In the Burgess model, which zone around the CBD contains old factories and poor housing?
A: The zone in transition (inner city)
Q: In a typical LIC or NEE city, where are squatter settlements usually found?
A: On the periphery or on hazardous land such as floodplains and steep slopes
Q: Why do the rich in many LIC cities live near the centre, unlike in HIC cities?
A: The centre has the best services, jobs and security
Q: What is social segregation in a city?
A: The separation of groups by income, ethnicity or class into different areas
Q: What is meant by cultural diversity in a city?
A: The presence of people of many ethnicities, languages, religions and lifestyles
Q: What is an ethnic enclave?
A: An area where one ethnic group is concentrated, with its own shops and institutions
Q: Why do new migrants often cluster in inner city areas?
A: Cheap rented housing and support from people of the same culture
Q: What is a town centre mixed development?
A: A scheme combining shops, offices, housing and leisure in one central site
Q: What is a cultural quarter?
A: An area of a city where arts, media and creative businesses are concentrated
Q: What is a heritage quarter?
A: An area where historic buildings are conserved and reused, often for tourism
Q: What is a fortress development?
A: A development designed to exclude outsiders using security such as walls, gates and CCTV
Q: What is a gated community?
A: A residential area with controlled entrances and boundary walls or fences
Q: What is hostile (defensive) architecture?
A: Design such as spikes or sloping benches that stops homeless people resting
Q: Which architect developed the 'defensible space' idea in 1972, linking design to crime levels?
A: Oscar Newman
Q: What is an edge city?
A: A large concentration of offices, retail and leisure at the edge of a city, beyond the old centre
Q: Which journalist defined the edge city in his 1991 book 'Edge City: Life on the New Frontier'?
A: Joel Garreau
Q: In Garreau's definition, how does an edge city's number of jobs compare with its number of bedrooms?
A: It has more jobs than bedrooms
Q: Why are edge cities usually located near motorway junctions?
A: They depend on car access for workers and shoppers
Q: What is the post-modern western city?
A: A fragmented, multi-centred city of varied architecture, consumption and segregated enclaves
Q: How does the post-modern city differ from the modernist city?
A: It has many centres and mixed styles rather than one CBD and planned zones
Q: Which US city is the model for the post-modern city studied by the 'LA School' of urbanists?
A: Los Angeles
Q: Which writer described 'Fortress L.A.' in his 1990 book 'City of Quartz'?
A: Mike Davis
Q: What characteristic of megacities in LICs is shown by large informal settlements?
A: Rapid growth that outpaces formal housing supply
Q: What is the informal economy?
A: Work that is not taxed, registered or regulated, e.g. street vending

## AQA_GEOG_3.2.3.4 | content
Q: What is the urban heat island effect?
A: Urban areas being warmer than the surrounding countryside
Q: Which amateur meteorologist first described London's heat island in 'The Climate of London' (1818 to 1820)?
A: Luke Howard
Q: At what time of day is the urban heat island usually most intense?
A: At night
Q: In what weather conditions is the urban heat island strongest?
A: Calm, clear anticyclonic conditions
Q: Why do concrete, brick and tarmac add to the urban heat island?
A: They absorb and store heat by day and release it slowly at night
Q: How does low albedo of dark urban surfaces add to the heat island?
A: Dark surfaces reflect less and absorb more solar radiation
Q: What is anthropogenic heat?
A: Heat released by human activity (e.g. vehicles, industry, heating, air conditioning)
Q: Why does less vegetation in cities raise temperatures?
A: Less evapotranspiration, so less energy is used as latent heat
Q: How do street canyons trap heat?
A: Tall buildings reflect radiation between walls and reduce the sky view for heat loss at night
Q: How does a pollution dome affect urban temperatures at night?
A: It absorbs and re-radiates outgoing longwave radiation back down
Q: Why does rapid drainage of rainwater raise urban temperatures?
A: Less surface water is left to evaporate and cool the city
Q: On a temperature cross-section, what is the sharp rise in temperature at the rural-urban edge called?
A: The cliff
Q: On a temperature cross-section, where is the peak of the urban heat island?
A: Over the dense city centre (CBD)
Q: What are parks and lakes called on an urban temperature cross-section, where temperatures dip?
A: Cool islands (sinks)
Q: How does the urban heat island affect snow cover in cities?
A: Snow falls and lies less often than in the countryside
Q: How does a green roof reduce the urban heat island?
A: Plants cool the roof by evapotranspiration and shading
Q: What is a cool roof?
A: A roof with a high-albedo (light) surface that reflects sunlight
Q: Roughly how much more rainfall do large cities receive than surrounding rural areas?
A: About 5 to 15% more
Q: How does the rough surface of a city increase rainfall?
A: Buildings cause turbulence that forces air to rise
Q: How does the urban heat island cause thunderstorms?
A: Strong convection lifts warm moist air, forming cumulonimbus clouds
Q: In which season and time of day are urban thunderstorms most common?
A: Summer, in the late afternoon and early evening
Q: Why does urban pollution increase precipitation?
A: Particulates act as condensation nuclei for water droplets
Q: Why were fogs more frequent in industrial cities before clean air laws?
A: Smoke particles acted as hygroscopic nuclei for condensation
Q: Why is fog now less frequent in many city centres than in nearby countryside?
A: The heat island keeps air above its dew point and pollution is lower
Q: How does the rough urban surface affect average wind speed?
A: Friction from buildings reduces average wind speed
Q: What is the venturi effect in cities?
A: Wind is squeezed and accelerated through gaps between tall buildings
Q: What is the canyon effect on urban wind?
A: Wind is channelled along streets lined by tall buildings
Q: What happens to wind that hits the face of a tall building?
A: It is deflected down the building, causing strong gusts at street level
Q: What forms on the sheltered (lee) side of tall buildings?
A: Eddies and vortices with turbulent, swirling air
Q: How does urban layout affect wind direction at street level?
A: Buildings divert wind along streets, so local direction differs from the regional wind
Q: What is a country breeze?
A: A light inflow of cooler rural air towards a warm city centre on calm nights
Q: What are particulates?
A: Tiny solid or liquid particles suspended in air, e.g. soot and dust
Q: What does PM2.5 mean?
A: Particulate matter less than 2.5 micrometres across
Q: Why is PM2.5 more harmful than larger particles?
A: It penetrates deep into the lungs and bloodstream
Q: Which source produces much urban particulate pollution in HIC cities?
A: Road traffic, especially diesel exhausts, plus brake and tyre wear
Q: Which pollutants react in sunlight to form photochemical smog?
A: Nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds
Q: Which secondary pollutant is the main component of photochemical smog?
A: Ground-level ozone
Q: In what weather conditions does photochemical smog form?
A: Hot, sunny, calm anticyclonic weather
Q: How does a temperature inversion worsen urban air pollution?
A: Warm air above cooler air stops pollutants rising and dispersing
Q: Why are cities in basins, such as Mexico City and Los Angeles, prone to smog?
A: Surrounding mountains trap air and inversions are frequent
Q: What is a congestion charge?
A: A daily fee to drive into a city's central zone, reducing traffic and pollution
Q: What is a low emission zone?
A: An area where the most polluting vehicles pay a charge or are banned
Q: What is one weakness of number-plate driving bans?
A: Wealthier people buy a second car to get round them
Q: How does a catalytic converter reduce urban pollution?
A: It converts carbon monoxide, NOx and hydrocarbons into less harmful gases

## AQA_GEOG_3.2.3.5 | content
Q: Why does urban surface runoff increase compared with rural areas?
A: Impermeable surfaces such as tarmac and roofs stop infiltration
Q: How do drains and sewers change water movement in an urban catchment?
A: They carry water rapidly to rivers, shortening travel time
Q: Which drainage basin store is greatly reduced by urbanisation?
A: Soil and groundwater storage (also interception by vegetation)
Q: How does urbanisation affect groundwater recharge?
A: Less infiltration and percolation reduce recharge
Q: What is channelisation?
A: Straightening, deepening or lining a river channel, often with concrete
Q: What is a culvert?
A: An enclosed channel or pipe that carries a river underground
Q: What is the effect of urbanisation on the lag time of a flood hydrograph?
A: It shortens the lag time
Q: What is the effect of urbanisation on peak discharge?
A: It raises peak discharge
Q: What shape is the rising limb on an urban storm hydrograph?
A: Steep
Q: What happens to the falling limb on an urban storm hydrograph?
A: It is steep, as water drains away quickly
Q: How does urbanisation affect a river's base flow?
A: It lowers base flow because less water reaches groundwater
Q: What is a combined sewer?
A: A sewer carrying both rainwater and sewage in the same pipe
Q: What is a combined sewer overflow (CSO)?
A: A release point where sewage and stormwater spill into rivers when sewers are full
Q: What is surface water (pluvial) flooding?
A: Flooding when intense rain overwhelms drains before reaching a river
Q: How does urban runoff affect river water quality?
A: It carries oil, metals, litter and sediment into rivers
Q: What are SUDS?
A: Sustainable urban drainage systems that mimic natural drainage by storing and slowing runoff
Q: In the SUDS triangle, what is the third aim besides water quantity and water quality?
A: Amenity and biodiversity
Q: What is the SUDS management train?
A: A sequence of techniques from source control through site to regional control
Q: How does a green roof work as a SUDS technique?
A: Plants and soil on the roof intercept and store rain, slowing runoff
Q: How does permeable paving work as a SUDS technique?
A: Rain soaks through gaps into a gravel layer below instead of running off
Q: What is a swale?
A: A shallow, grassed channel that carries, slows and infiltrates runoff
Q: What is a detention basin?
A: A dry grassy hollow that fills briefly in storms and releases water slowly
Q: What is a retention pond?
A: A permanent pond that stores runoff and lets pollutants settle
Q: What is a soakaway?
A: An underground pit filled with rubble that lets water infiltrate into the soil
Q: What is a rain garden?
A: A planted hollow that collects roof or road runoff and lets it soak in
Q: What is a filter strip?
A: A gently sloping strip of grass that slows runoff and traps sediment
Q: How do water butts reduce urban runoff?
A: They store roof water for reuse instead of sending it to drains
Q: What is one environmental benefit of SUDS beyond flood control?
A: They create wildlife habitats and green spaces
Q: What is one weakness of permeable paving?
A: Its gaps clog with silt and need regular cleaning
Q: Why are infiltration SUDS unsuitable on clay soils?
A: Clay has low permeability, so water cannot soak away
Q: Why should infiltration SUDS not be used on contaminated land?
A: Infiltrating water could carry pollutants into groundwater
Q: What is one economic weakness of retrofitting SUDS into existing cities?
A: Space is limited and installation is costly
Q: What is river restoration?
A: Returning a damaged river towards a more natural state and function
Q: What is de-culverting (daylighting)?
A: Opening up a river that was in an underground culvert
Q: Why does re-meandering a straightened river reduce flood risk downstream?
A: A longer, rougher channel slows water and increases lag time
Q: How does reconnecting a river to its floodplain reduce urban flooding?
A: The floodplain stores floodwater temporarily
Q: Why are weirs removed in river restoration projects?
A: To let fish migrate and restore natural flow and sediment movement
Q: What is one way of evaluating the outcome of a river restoration project?
A: Measuring changes in flood frequency, water quality and species numbers before and after
Q: What is one common conflict in urban river restoration projects?
A: Restoration needs land wanted for housing or other uses
