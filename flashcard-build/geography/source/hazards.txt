## AQA_GEOG_3.1.5.1 | content
Q: What is a natural hazard?
A: A natural event or process that threatens people, property or the environment
Q: What is a disaster, as opposed to a hazard?
A: A hazard event that causes major loss of life or damage beyond a community's ability to cope
Q: What is the term for a hazard caused by the Earth's internal processes, such as earthquakes and volcanoes?
A: Geophysical hazard
Q: What is the term for a hazard caused by weather and climate processes, such as tropical storms?
A: Atmospheric hazard
Q: What is the term for a hazard caused by the movement of water, such as river floods?
A: Hydrological hazard
Q: In hazard studies, what is vulnerability?
A: How likely people or places are to suffer harm from a hazard, and how badly
Q: In hazard studies, what is resilience?
A: The ability of a community to absorb, cope with and recover from a hazard event
Q: In the disaster risk equation, risk equals hazard multiplied by vulnerability, divided by what?
A: Capacity to cope
Q: What is the term for how people view and understand the threat posed by a hazard?
A: Hazard perception
Q: Why do wealthier people often perceive a hazard as less threatening than poorer people do?
A: They afford protection, insurance and recovery, so feel less exposed
Q: Why do some poorer people knowingly live in high-risk areas such as floodplains or volcano slopes?
A: Economic need, such as fertile land or jobs, outweighs the perceived risk
Q: Which cultural belief leads some communities to see hazards as acts of God or fate?
A: Religious or spiritual belief (leading to fatalism)
Q: How does recent personal experience of a hazard event usually affect hazard perception?
A: It raises perceived risk, so people take the threat more seriously
Q: How does a long gap since the last hazard event usually affect hazard perception?
A: People become complacent and underestimate the risk
Q: Which human response to hazards means accepting them as inevitable and doing little or nothing to reduce them?
A: Fatalism
Q: Which human response to hazards uses scientific monitoring to forecast when and where an event will happen?
A: Prediction
Q: Which human response to hazards means changing behaviour or the built environment to live with the hazard?
A: Adjustment (adaptation)
Q: Which human response to hazards means actions taken to reduce the severity of a hazard's impact?
A: Mitigation
Q: Which human response to hazards is the coordinated work of authorities before, during and after an event?
A: Management
Q: Which human response to hazards spreads the cost of losses across many people, for example through insurance?
A: Risk sharing
Q: Why is fatalism more common in low-income communities?
A: People lack the money, technology and education to reduce the risk
Q: Why is risk sharing through insurance more common in high-income countries?
A: More people afford premiums and insurance markets are well developed
Q: How does a high incidence (frequent events) of a hazard usually affect how people respond to it?
A: Frequent events encourage adaptation and preparedness as people learn to live with them
Q: Why do rare, very high-magnitude hazard events often overwhelm hazard management?
A: Defences and plans are designed for expected events, so extreme ones exceed them
Q: What is a primary impact of a hazard?
A: A direct effect of the hazard itself, such as buildings collapsing from ground shaking
Q: What is a secondary impact of a hazard?
A: An effect that results from the primary impacts, such as disease or fires
Q: Which geographer devised the disaster response curve that shows how quality of life changes after a hazard?
A: Chris Park (the Park model, 1991)
Q: What is another name for the Park model?
A: The disaster response curve
Q: What does the vertical axis of the Park model show?
A: Quality of life (level of normality)
Q: What does the horizontal axis of the Park model show?
A: Time
Q: In the Park model, what is stage 1, before the hazard event?
A: Pre-disaster (normality, with some risk and preparation)
Q: In the Park model, what is the stage when the hazard strikes and quality of life falls sharply?
A: Disruption
Q: In the Park model, what is the stage of rescue, emergency aid and medical care in the hours and days after an event?
A: Relief
Q: In the Park model, what is the stage when temporary shelter and services are restored over weeks or months?
A: Rehabilitation
Q: In the Park model, what is the final stage of rebuilding over months or years?
A: Reconstruction
Q: In the Park model, what does a curve that ends above the pre-disaster level show?
A: Quality of life improved because rebuilding was better than before
Q: In the Park model, what does a curve that ends below the pre-disaster level show?
A: The community never fully recovered, so quality of life stayed lower
Q: In the Park model, what does a very steep drop in the curve show?
A: A sudden, severe event with a rapid fall in quality of life
Q: In the Park model, what does a long, deep trough show?
A: Slow recovery, typical of poorer places with little aid or capacity
Q: What is one strength of the Park model for hazard managers?
A: It lets the recovery of different events be compared and planned for
Q: What is one weakness of the Park model?
A: It is generalised and does not show different groups recovering at different rates
Q: Which model shows hazard management as a continuous loop of mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery?
A: The hazard management cycle
Q: In the hazard management cycle, which stage involves planning, education and warning systems before an event?
A: Preparedness
Q: In the hazard management cycle, which stage involves deploying emergency services to save lives during and just after an event?
A: Response
Q: In the hazard management cycle, which stage involves rebuilding and restoring the built and natural environment?
A: Recovery
Q: In the hazard management cycle, which stage involves long-term steps such as building codes to reduce future impacts?
A: Mitigation
Q: Why is the hazard management cycle drawn as a loop?
A: Lessons from recovery feed into mitigation and preparedness for the next event
Q: What is one weakness of the hazard management cycle compared with the Park model?
A: It does not show timescales or how quality of life changes
Q: In what way does the hazard management cycle differ from the Park model in its focus on expertise?
A: The cycle includes the role of experts and planners, which Park's model does not

## AQA_GEOG_3.1.5.2 | content
Q: What is the solid, innermost layer of the Earth, made mainly of iron and nickel?
A: The inner core
Q: Which layer of the Earth is liquid iron and nickel?
A: The outer core
Q: What is the term for the rigid outer shell of the Earth, made of the crust and uppermost mantle?
A: The lithosphere
Q: What is the term for the hot, partly molten, plastic layer of the upper mantle on which plates move?
A: The asthenosphere
Q: Which type of crust is thinner, denser and made mainly of basalt?
A: Oceanic crust
Q: Which type of crust is thicker, less dense and made mainly of granite?
A: Continental crust
Q: Which type of crust is geologically older on average, oceanic or continental?
A: Continental crust (oceanic crust is recycled by subduction)
Q: What is the main source of the Earth's internal heat?
A: Radioactive decay of elements in the mantle and core
Q: Apart from radioactive decay, what is the other source of the Earth's internal heat?
A: Primordial heat left over from the Earth's formation
Q: Which scientist proposed continental drift and the supercontinent Pangaea?
A: Alfred Wegener
Q: What is the name of the supercontinent that Wegener said once joined all the continents?
A: Pangaea
Q: Which piece of evidence for continental drift is the matching coastline shapes of South America and Africa?
A: The jigsaw fit
Q: How do fossils support continental drift?
A: The same fossil species are found on continents now separated by oceans
Q: Which scientist proposed sea-floor spreading in the early 1960s?
A: Harry Hess
Q: What is sea-floor spreading?
A: New oceanic crust forms at ridges from rising magma and moves away on either side
Q: What is palaeomagnetism?
A: The record of the Earth's past magnetic field preserved in cooled volcanic rock
Q: How does palaeomagnetism support sea-floor spreading?
A: Magnetic stripes are mirrored on either side of ocean ridges
Q: How does rock age change with distance from an ocean ridge?
A: Rock gets older the further it is from the ridge
Q: Which scientist proposed that convection currents in the mantle move the plates?
A: Arthur Holmes
Q: How do convection currents drive plate movement?
A: Hot mantle rises and spreads, then cools and sinks, dragging plates above it
Q: What is ridge push?
A: Gravity makes new, raised lithosphere at a ridge slide away down the slope
Q: What is slab pull?
A: The weight of a dense, sinking oceanic plate pulls the rest of the plate behind it
Q: Which driving force is now thought to be the most important cause of plate movement?
A: Slab pull
Q: What is the term for plate movement driven by gravity as plates slide off raised ocean ridges?
A: Gravitational sliding
Q: What is another name for a destructive plate margin?
A: A convergent margin
Q: What is another name for a constructive plate margin?
A: A divergent margin
Q: What is another name for a conservative plate margin?
A: A transform margin
Q: At an oceanic-continental destructive margin, why does the oceanic plate subduct?
A: It is denser than the continental plate
Q: At an oceanic-oceanic destructive margin, which plate subducts?
A: The older, denser plate
Q: What is the term for the zone of earthquakes along a subducting plate, getting deeper inland?
A: The Benioff zone
Q: Why does the mantle melt above a subducting plate?
A: Water released from the slab lowers the melting point of the mantle
Q: Why do destructive margins produce explosive volcanoes?
A: Magma is high in silica and gas, so it is viscous and traps pressure
Q: Which type of magma, low in silica and runny, erupts at constructive margins and hotspots?
A: Basaltic magma
Q: Which type of magma, high in silica and viscous, erupts at destructive margins?
A: Andesitic (or rhyolitic) magma
Q: Why do the most powerful earthquakes occur at destructive margins?
A: Plates lock in the subduction zone, so huge stress builds before release
Q: Why are there no volcanoes at a conservative margin?
A: Crust is neither created nor destroyed, so no magma is produced
Q: What causes earthquakes at a conservative margin?
A: Plates stick as they slide past, then stress is released suddenly
Q: Why are earthquakes at constructive margins usually shallow and fairly weak?
A: The crust is thin and stress is released as plates pull apart
Q: Which landform forms where an oceanic plate bends down into the mantle?
A: A deep sea trench
Q: How do young fold mountains form?
A: Sediments between converging plates are compressed, folded and uplifted
Q: Which type of plate margin forms fold mountains with little or no volcanic activity?
A: Continental-continental (collision) margin
Q: How does an island arc form?
A: Magma rising above a subducting plate builds a curved line of volcanic islands
Q: At which type of plate margin do island arcs form?
A: Oceanic-oceanic destructive margin
Q: What common mistake do students make about island arcs?
A: Confusing them with hotspot island chains, which form over magma plumes
Q: How does an ocean ridge form?
A: Magma rises where plates diverge and builds a long underwater mountain range
Q: Which landform forms where continental crust is pulled apart at a constructive margin?
A: A rift valley
Q: How does a rift valley form?
A: Tension creates parallel faults and the block between them drops down
Q: What is the term for a block of land that drops between two parallel faults?
A: A graben
Q: What is a magma plume?
A: A column of hot mantle rising from deep in the Earth, away from plate margins
Q: What is the surface area of volcanic activity above a magma plume called?
A: A hotspot
Q: Why does a magma plume produce a chain of volcanoes?
A: The plate moves over a fixed plume, so new volcanoes form as old ones move away
Q: In a hotspot island chain, how does island age change with distance from the hotspot?
A: Islands get older and more eroded further from the hotspot
Q: What does a hotspot island chain show about the plate it sits on?
A: The direction and speed of plate movement
Q: What is the focus (hypocentre) of an earthquake?
A: The point underground where the rupture starts
Q: What is the epicentre of an earthquake?
A: The point on the surface directly above the focus

## AQA_GEOG_3.1.5.3 | content
Q: Where do most of the world's volcanoes occur?
A: Along plate margins, especially destructive margins around the Pacific
Q: What is the name for the belt of volcanoes and earthquakes around the Pacific Ocean?
A: The Pacific Ring of Fire
Q: What is a pyroclastic flow?
A: A fast-moving current of hot gas, ash and rock that flows down a volcano
Q: What is a nuée ardente?
A: A glowing cloud of superheated gas and ash, a type of pyroclastic flow
Q: Why are pyroclastic flows so deadly?
A: They move very fast at hundreds of degrees Celsius, so people cannot outrun them
Q: Why do basaltic lava flows rarely kill people?
A: They usually move slowly enough for people to walk away
Q: What is the main impact of lava flows?
A: Destruction of buildings, roads and farmland in their path
Q: What is a lahar?
A: A volcanic mudflow of water, ash and rock that flows down river valleys
Q: What triggers a lahar during an eruption?
A: Rapid melting of snow and ice on the volcano
Q: What triggers a lahar after an eruption?
A: Heavy rain, such as from a tropical storm, washing loose ash downslope
Q: What is tephra?
A: Any rock fragments thrown into the air by an eruption
Q: What size are volcanic bombs?
A: Larger than 64 mm
Q: What size are lapilli?
A: Between 2 mm and 64 mm
Q: What size is volcanic ash?
A: Smaller than 2 mm
Q: Why does heavy ashfall cause roofs to collapse?
A: Ash is heavy, especially when wet from rain
Q: Why is volcanic ash dangerous to aircraft?
A: It melts inside jet engines and makes them fail
Q: What is one health impact of volcanic ash fallout?
A: Breathing problems from inhaling fine ash
Q: Which volcanic gas reacts with water vapour to form acid rain?
A: Sulphur dioxide
Q: Why is volcanic carbon dioxide dangerous in valleys and hollows?
A: It is denser than air, so it collects in low ground and suffocates people
Q: How do large explosive eruptions cool global climate?
A: Sulphur dioxide forms aerosols in the stratosphere that reflect sunlight
Q: What is a jökulhlaup?
A: A sudden flood caused by an eruption melting ice beneath a glacier
Q: What does the Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) measure?
A: The size of an eruption, from the volume of material ejected and plume height
Q: What is the range of the Volcanic Explosivity Index?
A: 0 to 8
Q: How many times more material does each step up the VEI represent?
A: About 10 times more (it is logarithmic)
Q: Which type of volcano has gentle slopes and is built from runny basaltic lava?
A: A shield volcano
Q: Which type of volcano is steep-sided and built from layers of ash and lava?
A: A composite volcano (stratovolcano)
Q: Why are composite volcanoes at destructive margins less regular in their eruptions?
A: Long dormant periods let gas pressure build before large explosions
Q: What is an effusive eruption?
A: A gentle eruption where runny lava flows out
Q: Why are volcanic eruptions more predictable than earthquakes?
A: Rising magma gives warning signs such as tremors, swelling and gas release
Q: What is a primary impact of a volcanic eruption?
A: An impact caused directly by the eruption, such as burial by a pyroclastic flow
Q: What is a secondary impact of a volcanic eruption?
A: An impact that follows the eruption, such as lahars or famine
Q: What is one long-term economic benefit of living near a volcano?
A: Fertile volcanic soils for farming (also: geothermal energy, tourism)
Q: Which monitoring instrument detects the small earthquakes caused by magma rising?
A: A seismometer
Q: What is harmonic tremor?
A: Continuous ground vibration caused by magma moving, often a sign of an eruption
Q: Which instruments detect a volcano's ground swelling as magma rises?
A: Tiltmeters (also: GPS receivers)
Q: Which satellite technique maps ground deformation on volcanoes from space?
A: InSAR (satellite radar interferometry)
Q: What change in gas emissions warns that magma is nearing the surface?
A: An increase in sulphur dioxide released
Q: How does thermal imaging help predict eruptions?
A: It detects rising ground temperature as magma approaches
Q: Why is it impossible to prevent a volcanic eruption?
A: The forces driving magma to the surface are far too large to control
Q: Which mitigation method uses walls, channels or water spraying to protect settlements from lava?
A: Lava diversion
Q: Which engineering structures slow down or trap lahars in river valleys?
A: Sabo dams (also: lahar channels, dykes)
Q: How does building design reduce the impact of ash fallout?
A: Steep roofs that shed ash and strong roof supports
Q: Which preparedness method marks an area around a volcano that people must leave?
A: An exclusion zone
Q: How do hazard maps help prepare for an eruption?
A: They show areas at risk from flows, so land use and evacuation are planned
Q: How do volcano alert levels help preparedness?
A: They tell people and officials how close an eruption is and when to evacuate
Q: Why do some people stay in danger zones despite eruption warnings?
A: Fear of losing homes, crops or livestock, or distrust of warnings

## AQA_GEOG_3.1.5.4 | content
Q: What is an earthquake?
A: Ground shaking caused by the sudden release of built-up stress along a fault
Q: What is elastic rebound theory?
A: Rocks bend as stress builds on a locked fault, then snap back when it breaks
Q: What depth range counts as a shallow-focus earthquake?
A: 0 to 70 km deep
Q: Why do shallow-focus earthquakes usually cause more damage than deep ones?
A: Seismic waves lose less energy before reaching the surface
Q: Which seismic waves are fastest and travel through both solids and liquids?
A: P waves (primary waves)
Q: How do P waves move the ground?
A: By compression, pushing and pulling rock in the direction of travel
Q: Which seismic waves are shear waves that only travel through solids?
A: S waves (secondary waves)
Q: Which seismic waves travel along the ground surface and cause most damage?
A: Surface waves (Love and Rayleigh waves)
Q: Which surface waves shake the ground from side to side?
A: Love waves
Q: Which surface waves give a rolling motion like ocean waves?
A: Rayleigh waves
Q: What is an aftershock?
A: A smaller earthquake that follows the main shock as the crust adjusts
Q: Which earthquake scale is now used by USGS, based on fault area, slip and rock rigidity?
A: The moment magnitude scale (Mw)
Q: Which early logarithmic earthquake scale was based on wave amplitude on a seismometer?
A: The Richter scale
Q: How much bigger is the wave amplitude of a magnitude 7 earthquake than a magnitude 6?
A: 10 times bigger
Q: Roughly how much more energy does a magnitude 7 earthquake release than a magnitude 6?
A: About 32 times more
Q: Roughly how much more energy does a magnitude 8 earthquake release than a magnitude 6?
A: About 1,000 times more (32 x 32)
Q: Which scale measures earthquake intensity from observed effects and damage, using Roman numerals I to XII?
A: The Modified Mercalli Intensity scale
Q: Why does one earthquake have one magnitude but many Mercalli intensity values?
A: Intensity varies with distance, geology and building quality at each place
Q: How is the frequency of earthquakes related to their magnitude?
A: Low-magnitude earthquakes are far more frequent than high-magnitude ones
Q: What is meant by the randomness of earthquakes?
A: Their location along margins is known but their timing is not
Q: What is an intraplate earthquake?
A: An earthquake far from a plate margin, often on an old fault
Q: What is liquefaction?
A: Saturated loose sediment loses strength when shaken and behaves like a liquid
Q: Which ground conditions are most prone to liquefaction?
A: Saturated, loose, sandy soils (such as reclaimed land and river deposits)
Q: What is one impact of liquefaction on buildings?
A: Buildings sink, tilt or collapse as the ground loses strength
Q: What common mistake do students make about liquefaction?
A: Treating it as a mantle process rather than a ground hazard from shaking
Q: How do earthquakes trigger landslides?
A: Shaking loosens material on steep slopes, which then slides downhill
Q: What usually causes a tsunami?
A: An undersea earthquake that suddenly lifts the seabed and displaces water
Q: At which type of plate margin do most large tsunami-causing earthquakes occur?
A: Destructive (subduction) margins
Q: Why is a tsunami hard to notice in the deep ocean?
A: Its wave height is small and its wavelength is very long
Q: Why does a tsunami grow taller as it reaches the coast?
A: It slows in shallow water, so the wave bunches up and rises
Q: What natural warning sign often comes just before a tsunami reaches land?
A: The sea suddenly drawing back from the shore
Q: Apart from earthquakes, what else triggers tsunamis?
A: Underwater landslides (also: volcanic eruptions)
Q: Why is it not possible to predict the exact time of an earthquake?
A: There are no reliable warning signs of when a locked fault will rupture
Q: What is seismic gap theory?
A: Parts of an active fault that have not moved for a long time are most likely to rupture next
Q: How does an earthquake early warning system work?
A: It detects fast P waves and warns before slower, damaging waves arrive
Q: How much warning does an earthquake early warning system typically give?
A: Seconds to tens of seconds
Q: What do DART buoys detect?
A: Changes in pressure on the deep seabed caused by passing tsunami waves
Q: Which aseismic design feature uses rubber bearings so a building moves less than the ground?
A: Base isolation
Q: How does cross-bracing make a building safer in an earthquake?
A: It stiffens the frame so it resists sideways shaking
Q: What is a tuned mass damper?
A: A heavy weight at the top of a tall building that moves to counter swaying
Q: What is retrofitting?
A: Strengthening existing buildings to resist earthquakes
Q: Why do building codes only reduce earthquake deaths if enforced?
A: Unenforced codes let unsafe buildings be built and occupied
Q: How does land-use zoning reduce seismic risk?
A: It keeps key buildings off land prone to liquefaction, landslides or tsunami
Q: How do earthquake drills reduce deaths?
A: People learn to drop, cover and hold on instead of panicking
Q: Which coastal structures reduce the impact of tsunamis?
A: Sea walls (also: tsunami evacuation towers)
Q: Why are earthquakes in low-income countries usually more deadly than in high-income countries?
A: Poorly built housing collapses and rescue resources are limited
