## AQA_GEOG_3.1.3.1 | content
Q: In physical geography, what is a system?
A: A set of linked parts (components) that work together and exchange energy and matter
Q: Why is a coast described as an open system?
A: It receives inputs from outside and loses outputs beyond its boundaries
Q: What is a closed system?
A: A system that transfers energy but not matter across its boundary
Q: In the coastal system, which type of energy do wind and waves supply?
A: Kinetic energy
Q: In the coastal system, which type of energy does cliff material store because of its height above the beach?
A: Potential energy (gravitational)
Q: Apart from energy, what is the main material input to the coastal system?
A: Sediment (from rivers, cliff erosion, the sea bed and wind)
Q: In the coastal system, what term is given to beaches, dunes, spits and offshore bars?
A: Stores (sediment stores or components)
Q: In systems terms, what is longshore drift an example of?
A: A flow or transfer of sediment
Q: In the coastal system, what is an example of an output of sediment?
A: Sediment carried offshore into deep water or out of the sediment cell
Q: What is negative feedback in a system?
A: A change that triggers effects which cancel it out, returning the system towards balance
Q: What is positive feedback in a system?
A: A change that triggers effects which amplify it, pushing the system further from balance
Q: How does a storm that strips sand from a beach show negative feedback?
A: The sand forms an offshore bar that makes waves break earlier, protecting the beach
Q: How does trampling of marram grass on a dune show positive feedback?
A: Bare sand is blown away, more roots are exposed and more vegetation dies, forming a blowout
Q: How does a widening wave-cut platform show negative feedback?
A: Waves break further out and lose energy, so cliff retreat slows
Q: What is dynamic equilibrium in a coastal system?
A: A balance between inputs and outputs so the system stays stable while constantly adjusting
Q: What happens to a coast in dynamic equilibrium after a storm alters its beach?
A: Processes adjust the beach back towards its equilibrium form
Q: What is a landform?
A: An individual feature of the land with a distinctive shape made by particular processes
Q: What is a landscape?
A: A whole area whose character comes from a group of related landforms
Q: Which type of coastal landscape is made up of cliffs, caves, arches, stacks and wave-cut platforms?
A: An erosional (high-energy) coastal landscape
Q: Which type of coastal landscape is made up of beaches, spits, dunes and saltmarshes?
A: A depositional (low-energy) coastal landscape
Q: Why does coastal management benefit from understanding feedback?
A: Working with negative feedback restores natural balance, while ignoring it can trigger damaging positive feedback

## AQA_GEOG_3.1.3.2 | content
Q: What is the main source of energy for waves?
A: Wind blowing over the sea surface (frictional drag)
Q: What is fetch?
A: The distance of open water over which the wind blows to create waves
Q: How does wind speed affect wave height?
A: Faster winds transfer more energy, making higher waves
Q: How does the length of time the wind blows affect wave height?
A: Wind blowing for longer builds higher waves
Q: What is a prevailing wind?
A: The most frequent wind direction at a place
Q: What is the prevailing wind direction in the UK?
A: South-westerly
Q: How do water particles move in a wave in open water?
A: In a circular (orbital) motion, with little forward movement of water
Q: Why does a wave break as it reaches shallow water?
A: Friction with the sea bed slows its base, so the crest gets higher and topples forward
Q: What happens to wavelength as a wave enters shallow water?
A: It decreases
Q: What is swash?
A: The movement of water up the beach after a wave breaks
Q: What is backwash?
A: The movement of water back down the beach under gravity
Q: Which is stronger in a constructive wave, swash or backwash?
A: Swash
Q: Which is stronger in a destructive wave, swash or backwash?
A: Backwash
Q: About how many constructive waves break per minute?
A: About 6 to 8
Q: About how many destructive waves break per minute?
A: About 11 to 15
Q: How does the height of a constructive wave compare with a destructive wave?
A: It is lower (usually under 1 m)
Q: Which type of wave builds up beaches?
A: Constructive waves
Q: Which type of wave removes sediment from beaches and carries it offshore?
A: Destructive waves
Q: Which beach feature at the top of the beach is built by constructive waves?
A: A berm
Q: What is wave refraction?
A: The bending of waves as they slow down in shallower water near the coast
Q: Where does wave refraction concentrate wave energy?
A: On headlands
Q: Where does wave refraction spread out wave energy?
A: In bays
Q: What causes tides?
A: The gravitational pull of the Moon (and to a lesser extent the Sun) on the oceans
Q: What is tidal range?
A: The vertical difference in height between high tide and low tide
Q: What type of tide occurs when the Sun, Moon and Earth are in line?
A: Spring tide (largest tidal range)
Q: What type of tide occurs when the Sun and Moon are at right angles to the Earth?
A: Neap tide (smallest tidal range)
Q: Why do tides matter for coastal erosion?
A: They move the zone where waves attack up and down the cliff or beach
Q: What is a storm surge?
A: A temporary rise in sea level caused by low air pressure and strong onshore winds
Q: Roughly how much does sea level rise for each 1 millibar fall in air pressure?
A: About 1 cm
Q: What is a rip current?
A: A strong, narrow current flowing seaward from the shore
Q: What is a longshore current?
A: A current that flows parallel to the shore, created by waves breaking at an angle
Q: What is a high-energy coast?
A: A coast with powerful waves where erosion exceeds deposition
Q: What is a low-energy coast?
A: A sheltered coast with gentle waves where deposition exceeds erosion
Q: How does deep water close to the shore affect wave energy?
A: Waves keep their energy until they reach the coast, so the coast is high energy
Q: Which type of wave dominates high-energy coasts?
A: Destructive waves
Q: Which landforms are typical of high-energy coasts?
A: Erosional landforms such as cliffs, headlands and wave-cut platforms
Q: Which landforms are typical of low-energy coasts?
A: Depositional landforms such as beaches, spits, mudflats and saltmarshes
Q: What is the largest source of sediment for most coasts?
A: Rivers
Q: How does cliff erosion act as a sediment source?
A: Eroded and collapsed cliff material falls onto the beach and is moved by waves
Q: How can the sea bed supply sediment to the coast?
A: Waves and tides move offshore sediment landwards
Q: What is a biological (biogenic) source of coastal sediment?
A: Shells and coral fragments
Q: What is a sediment cell?
A: A stretch of coast, often bounded by headlands, within which sediment largely circulates
Q: Why is a sediment cell treated as a closed system?
A: Little sediment moves between neighbouring cells
Q: What is a sediment budget?
A: The balance between sediment added to and removed from a sediment cell
Q: What happens to a coast with a positive sediment budget?
A: It builds out (accretion)
Q: What happens to a coast with a negative sediment budget?
A: It erodes and beaches shrink
Q: In a sediment cell, what is a sediment sink?
A: An area where sediment accumulates, such as a spit or offshore bank
Q: What is weathering?
A: The breakdown of rock in its original place (in situ)
Q: What is sub-aerial weathering?
A: Weathering by land-based processes (rain, frost, wind) rather than by waves
Q: What is freeze-thaw weathering?
A: Water in cracks freezes and expands by about 9 percent, widening the cracks
Q: What is salt crystallisation?
A: Salt crystals grow in rock pores as seawater evaporates, forcing the rock apart
Q: Which rocks are weakened most by wetting and drying?
A: Clay-rich rocks such as shale, which swell when wet and shrink when dry
Q: What is carbonation?
A: Weak carbonic acid in rainwater dissolves calcium carbonate in limestone and chalk
Q: What does carbonation turn calcium carbonate into?
A: Soluble calcium bicarbonate
Q: What is oxidation in weathering?
A: Oxygen reacting with minerals such as iron, weakening the rock
Q: What is hydrolysis in weathering?
A: A reaction between water and minerals (e.g. feldspar in granite) forming clay
Q: What is biological weathering at the coast?
A: Rock broken down by plants, animals or organisms such as burrowing piddocks
Q: What is mass movement?
A: The movement of material downslope under the influence of gravity
Q: How does heavy rain increase the risk of cliff mass movement?
A: It saturates the cliff, adding weight and reducing friction
Q: How does wave undercutting cause mass movement?
A: It removes support from the cliff base so the cliff above collapses
Q: What is a rockfall?
A: Rock fragments detaching from a steep cliff and falling freely
Q: What landform does repeated rockfall create at the cliff foot?
A: A scree (talus) slope
Q: What is a rotational slump?
A: A block of material sliding down a curved slip plane, rotating backwards as it moves
Q: On which cliff materials are rotational slumps most common?
A: Soft, clay-rich materials such as boulder clay (till)
Q: What landform does repeated rotational slumping leave on a cliff?
A: A stepped profile of back-tilted terraces
Q: What is a landslide (translational slide)?
A: Material sliding down a straight slip plane such as a bedding plane or joint
Q: What is a mudflow?
A: A rapid flow of saturated fine material down a slope
Q: What is soil creep?
A: Very slow movement of soil particles downslope as they are lifted and fall back
Q: What is solifluction?
A: Slow downslope flow of the thawed active layer over permafrost
Q: What is runoff on a coastal cliff?
A: Rainwater flowing over the cliff face, washing material down and cutting gullies
Q: What is hydraulic action?
A: Waves force water and air into cracks, and the pressure breaks the rock apart
Q: What is wave quarrying?
A: Breaking waves removing blocks of rock already loosened from the cliff face
Q: What is abrasion (corrasion)?
A: Waves throwing sediment against the cliff, wearing it away
Q: What is cavitation?
A: Air bubbles in breaking waves collapse, sending shockwaves that weaken rock
Q: What is solution (corrosion) as a marine erosion process?
A: Rock such as limestone or chalk dissolved by weak acids in seawater
Q: What is attrition?
A: Rock fragments knocking into each other, becoming smaller and rounder
Q: How does rock resistance (lithology) affect erosion rates?
A: Resistant rocks such as granite erode slowly, weak rocks such as clay erode quickly
Q: How do joints and faults affect the rate of coastal erosion?
A: They are lines of weakness that hydraulic action exploits
Q: How does a wide beach reduce cliff erosion?
A: Waves lose energy crossing it before reaching the cliff
Q: What is traction?
A: Large pebbles and boulders rolled along the sea bed
Q: What is saltation?
A: Small pebbles and sand bounced along the sea bed
Q: What is suspension?
A: Fine particles such as silt and clay carried within the water
Q: What is solution as a transport process?
A: Dissolved minerals carried in the water
Q: What is longshore (littoral) drift?
A: The movement of sediment along the coast by waves that approach at an angle
Q: In longshore drift, what controls the angle at which waves approach the beach?
A: The prevailing wind
Q: In longshore drift, how does swash move sediment?
A: Up the beach at the angle of the approaching waves
Q: In longshore drift, how does backwash move sediment?
A: Straight back down the beach at right angles, under gravity
Q: In longshore drift, what is the overall path of a pebble?
A: A zigzag along the beach
Q: When does coastal deposition happen?
A: When waves or currents lose energy and can no longer carry their load
Q: What is flocculation?
A: Fine clay particles clumping together in salt water and sinking
Q: Which particles are deposited first as water loses energy?
A: The largest and heaviest
Q: Why are shingle beaches steeper than sand beaches?
A: Swash percolates into the large gaps, so backwash is weak and leaves material behind

## AQA_GEOG_3.1.3.3 | content
Q: Where on a cliff does wave erosion mainly concentrate?
A: Between the high and low tide marks at the cliff base
Q: What is a wave-cut notch?
A: An indentation eroded at the base of a cliff by hydraulic action and abrasion
Q: What happens when a wave-cut notch grows large?
A: The overhanging cliff above becomes unstable and collapses
Q: What is a wave-cut platform?
A: A gently sloping rocky surface left at the base of a retreating cliff
Q: What is the typical slope of a wave-cut platform?
A: Less than about 4 degrees
Q: Why do wave-cut platforms rarely grow wider than about 0.5 km?
A: Waves break further out and lose energy before reaching the cliff
Q: What cliff profile forms where rock strata dip steeply towards the sea?
A: A sloping cliff prone to slides along the bedding planes
Q: What cliff profile forms where rock strata dip inland?
A: A steep cliff
Q: Why do boulder clay cliffs have a low, slumped profile?
A: The soft material slumps easily when saturated and is quickly eroded
Q: What is a discordant coastline?
A: A coast where bands of different rock run at right angles to the shore
Q: What landforms form on a discordant coastline?
A: Headlands of resistant rock and bays in weaker rock
Q: What is a concordant coastline?
A: A coast where bands of rock run parallel to the shore
Q: Which landform forms when waves break through resistant rock on a concordant coast?
A: A cove, as weaker rock behind is eroded
Q: In the stack sequence, where does erosion of a headland start?
A: At lines of weakness such as joints, faults or bedding planes
Q: In the stack sequence, what does an enlarged crack in a headland become?
A: A cave
Q: How does an arch form?
A: A cave erodes right through a headland (or two caves meet back to back)
Q: How does a stack form?
A: The roof of an arch collapses, leaving an isolated pillar of rock
Q: Which processes weaken the roof of an arch?
A: Sub-aerial weathering above and marine erosion below
Q: How does a stump form?
A: A stack is undercut at its base and collapses, leaving a low rock
Q: What is a blowhole?
A: A vertical shaft from a cave roof to the cliff top, through which spray is forced
Q: What is a geo?
A: A narrow, steep-sided inlet formed where a cave roof collapses along a joint
Q: What is a beach?
A: An accumulation of sand and shingle between the lowest tides and the highest storm waves
Q: What is a swash-aligned beach?
A: A beach built where waves arrive parallel to the coast, so little longshore drift occurs
Q: What is a drift-aligned beach?
A: A beach built where waves arrive at an angle, so longshore drift moves sediment along it
Q: What is a storm beach?
A: A ridge of large pebbles thrown to the top of a beach by storm waves
Q: What are beach cusps?
A: Semi-circular hollows of sand or shingle spaced evenly along a beach
Q: What are runnels on a beach?
A: Shallow troughs parallel to the shoreline between low sand ridges
Q: What is a spit?
A: A long narrow ridge of sand or shingle joined to land at one end and extending into the sea
Q: Where do spits begin to form?
A: Where the coastline changes direction, such as at a river mouth
Q: Why does a spit keep growing out from the coast?
A: Longshore drift keeps depositing sediment past the bend in the coast
Q: What makes the end of a spit curve (recurve)?
A: Wave refraction and a change in wind direction
Q: Why does a spit not grow right across an estuary?
A: The river current carries sediment away
Q: What forms in the sheltered water behind a spit?
A: Mudflats and saltmarsh
Q: What is a compound spit?
A: A spit with a series of recurved ridges marking former positions of its end
Q: What is a tombolo?
A: A ridge of sand or shingle joining an island to the mainland
Q: How does a tombolo form?
A: Waves refract around the island and meet behind it, depositing sediment
Q: What is an offshore bar?
A: A submerged ridge of sand or shingle running parallel to the coast offshore
Q: How does an offshore bar form?
A: Destructive waves drag sediment offshore and deposit it where they break
Q: What is a barrier beach (bar)?
A: A ridge of sediment that grows across a bay, cutting off a lagoon behind it
Q: What is a barrier island?
A: A long offshore island of sand parallel to the coast, separated from it by a lagoon
Q: How did many barrier islands form?
A: Rising sea levels after the last glacial pushed offshore sand ridges landwards
Q: What is the first requirement for sand dunes to form?
A: A large supply of sand on a wide beach
Q: Why does a large tidal range help sand dunes form?
A: A wide area of sand is exposed at low tide to dry out
Q: Which wind is needed to build coastal sand dunes?
A: A strong onshore wind
Q: By which process does wind move sand grains along the beach?
A: Saltation
Q: What starts an embryo dune?
A: Wind-blown sand piling up against an obstacle such as driftwood or a rock
Q: What is the name for plant succession on sand?
A: Psammosere
Q: Which plant colonises and stabilises yellow (fore) dunes?
A: Marram grass
Q: How does marram grass trap sand?
A: Its leaves slow the wind and its long roots bind the sand
Q: Why are grey dunes grey?
A: Decaying plants add humus to the sand
Q: How do pH and humus change from embryo dunes to grey dunes?
A: Humus increases and the soil becomes more acidic
Q: What is a dune slack?
A: A damp hollow between dune ridges where the water table reaches the surface
Q: What is a blowout?
A: A hollow where wind removes sand from a dune once vegetation is lost
Q: What is the climax vegetation at the back of a UK dune system?
A: Heath or woodland (e.g. oak or pine)
Q: What is the name for plant succession in salt water?
A: Halosere
Q: Where do saltmarshes form?
A: In sheltered, low-energy areas such as estuaries and behind spits
Q: Why is mud deposited where a river meets the incoming tide?
A: The water slows and loses energy, and clay flocculates in salt water
Q: What is a mudflat?
A: A flat area of fine sediment in an estuary exposed at low tide
Q: Which pioneer plant first colonises mudflats?
A: Glasswort (Salicornia) or cordgrass (Spartina)
Q: How do pioneer plants help a saltmarsh grow upward?
A: They slow the water and trap more sediment
Q: What happens as a saltmarsh surface rises above most high tides?
A: Less salt-tolerant plants can grow and succession continues
Q: What are saltmarsh creeks?
A: Channels that drain tidal water across the marsh surface
Q: What are salt pans on a saltmarsh?
A: Hollows where trapped seawater evaporates, leaving high salt levels
Q: Which forests replace saltmarsh on tropical sheltered coasts?
A: Mangroves
Q: What is eustatic sea level change?
A: A global change in sea level caused by a change in the volume of ocean water
Q: How did eustatic sea level change during a glacial period?
A: It fell as water was stored on land as ice
Q: How does thermal expansion cause eustatic sea level rise?
A: Warmer water expands and takes up more volume
Q: What is isostatic sea level change?
A: A local change in sea level caused by land rising or sinking
Q: What causes isostatic sinking of land during a glacial?
A: The weight of ice sheets presses the crust down
Q: What is isostatic rebound (post-glacial rebound)?
A: The slow rise of land after the weight of an ice sheet is removed
Q: What is tectonic sea level change?
A: A local change caused by earthquakes or plate movements raising or lowering land
Q: About how much lower was global sea level at the last glacial maximum than today?
A: About 120 m lower
Q: About when was the last glacial maximum?
A: About 20,000 years ago
Q: What name is given to the rise in sea level after the last glacial?
A: The Flandrian transgression
Q: What is a coastline of emergence?
A: A coast where land has risen relative to the sea
Q: What is a coastline of submergence?
A: A coast where the sea has risen relative to the land
Q: What is a raised beach?
A: A former beach left above the present high tide level after land rose
Q: What features are often found at the back of a raised beach?
A: Relict cliffs with old caves and wave-cut notches
Q: What is a marine platform (raised wave-cut platform)?
A: A former wave-cut platform left above present sea level
Q: What is a ria?
A: A drowned river valley flooded by rising sea level
Q: What is the shape of a ria in cross-section?
A: V-shaped (gentle sides), getting shallower inland
Q: What is a fjord?
A: A drowned glacial trough flooded by rising sea level
Q: Which glacial erosion processes carved the troughs that became fjords?
A: Abrasion and plucking
Q: What is the shape of a fjord in cross-section?
A: U-shaped with steep sides and very deep water
Q: What is the shallow ridge at the mouth of a fjord called?
A: A threshold (sill)
Q: Why do fjords have a threshold at the mouth?
A: Glacial erosion was weaker where the ice thinned and met the sea
Q: What is a Dalmatian coastline?
A: A coast where valleys parallel to the shore flooded, leaving long narrow islands
Q: How does rising sea level threaten saltmarshes?
A: Coastal squeeze, as they cannot move inland past sea walls or high land
Q: What is coastal squeeze?
A: Loss of intertidal habitat trapped between rising sea level and fixed defences or high ground
Q: How is climate change expected to affect storm patterns at the coast?
A: More intense storms bring larger waves and storm surges
Q: How will sea level rise affect cliff erosion?
A: Waves reach higher up cliffs more often, speeding up erosion
Q: How will sea level rise affect low-lying coasts?
A: More frequent flooding and permanent inundation
Q: What is a relict landform?
A: A landform made by processes no longer acting at that place, such as a raised beach
Q: Why do coastal landforms show the effects of time?
A: They take hundreds to thousands of years to form, so they show past as well as present processes
Q: How do single storms alter coastal landscapes?
A: They cause sudden large changes, such as arch collapse or spit breach

## AQA_GEOG_3.1.3.4 | content
Q: What is hard engineering?
A: Building artificial structures to stop or control natural coastal processes
Q: What is soft engineering?
A: Working with natural processes to reduce erosion and flood risk
Q: What is a sea wall?
A: A concrete wall along the coast that reflects wave energy
Q: Why is the face of a modern sea wall curved (recurved)?
A: To reflect waves back out to sea and stop water spilling over the top
Q: What is one advantage of a sea wall?
A: Very effective protection for high-value towns and seafronts
Q: What is one disadvantage of a sea wall?
A: Reflected waves scour the beach in front, undermining the wall
Q: What is a groyne?
A: A timber or rock barrier built at right angles to the beach to trap longshore drift
Q: What is one advantage of groynes?
A: They build a wider beach that absorbs wave energy
Q: What is one disadvantage of groynes?
A: They starve beaches further down the coast of sediment, increasing erosion there
Q: What is rock armour (rip-rap)?
A: Large boulders piled at the base of a cliff or sea wall to absorb wave energy
Q: What is one advantage of rock armour?
A: It is cheaper than a sea wall and absorbs wave energy well
Q: What is one disadvantage of rock armour?
A: Rock is often imported, it looks out of place and it is hard to walk over
Q: What is a revetment?
A: A sloping structure of concrete, timber or rock that breaks up and absorbs waves
Q: What is one disadvantage of a revetment?
A: It needs regular maintenance and restricts beach access
Q: What is a gabion?
A: A wire cage filled with rocks, stacked at the cliff base to absorb wave energy
Q: What is one disadvantage of gabions?
A: The wire cages corrode and break, so they last only a few years
Q: What is an offshore breakwater?
A: A barrier of rock built offshore so waves break before reaching the shore
Q: What is a tidal barrier?
A: A barrier with gates across an estuary that close to block storm surges
Q: What is beach nourishment (recharge)?
A: Adding sand or shingle to a beach, often dredged from offshore
Q: What is one advantage of beach nourishment?
A: It looks natural and widens the beach for tourism while absorbing wave energy
Q: What is one disadvantage of beach nourishment?
A: It must be repeated regularly as sediment is washed away
Q: What is beach reprofiling?
A: Reshaping the beach with machines to restore its natural profile after storms
Q: What is dune stabilisation?
A: Planting marram grass and fencing dunes to trap sand and reduce erosion
Q: What is one disadvantage of dune stabilisation?
A: Fencing restricts public access to the dunes
Q: What is cliff regrading?
A: Reducing the angle of a cliff to make it more stable
Q: What is cliff drainage?
A: Removing water from cliffs with pipes to reduce mass movement
Q: What is managed realignment?
A: Allowing the coastline to move inland by breaching old defences, often with new defences further back
Q: What is one advantage of managed realignment?
A: It creates saltmarsh that absorbs wave energy and provides wildlife habitat
Q: What is one disadvantage of managed realignment?
A: Farmland and property are lost to the sea
Q: How do mangrove forests reduce coastal flood risk?
A: Their roots and trunks slow water, reducing waves and storm surges
Q: What is a Shoreline Management Plan (SMP)?
A: A long-term plan setting how each stretch of coast will be managed
Q: On what natural units are Shoreline Management Plans based?
A: Sediment cells (and sub-cells)
Q: How far ahead do Shoreline Management Plans plan?
A: About 100 years
Q: What time span does the first (short-term) epoch of a Shoreline Management Plan cover?
A: 0 to 20 years
Q: What time span does the second (medium-term) epoch of a Shoreline Management Plan cover?
A: 20 to 50 years
Q: What time span does the third (long-term) epoch of a Shoreline Management Plan cover?
A: 50 to 100 years
Q: What does the SMP policy 'hold the line' mean?
A: Keep the shoreline where it is with defences
Q: What does the SMP policy 'advance the line' mean?
A: Build new defences seaward of the current shoreline
Q: What does the SMP policy 'managed realignment' mean?
A: Allow the shoreline to move, while managing the change
Q: What does the SMP policy 'no active intervention' mean?
A: No money is spent on defences, so nature takes its course
Q: Which SMP policy is usually chosen for undeveloped farmland cliffs?
A: No active intervention
Q: What is Integrated Coastal Zone Management (ICZM)?
A: Managing the whole coastal zone as one unit, involving all stakeholders, for long-term sustainability
Q: How does ICZM differ from older approaches to coastal defence?
A: It sees the coast as one connected system rather than defending places separately
Q: Why is ICZM considered sustainable?
A: It balances economic, social and environmental needs now and in future
Q: What is cost-benefit analysis in coastal management?
A: Comparing the cost of a scheme with the value of what it protects
Q: Why is hard engineering often seen as unsustainable?
A: It is costly to maintain and works against natural processes
Q: How can defending one stretch of coast harm another?
A: It interrupts sediment supply, increasing erosion down the coast
Q: What is terminal groyne syndrome?
A: Increased erosion just beyond the last groyne in a scheme
Q: Why do coastal management decisions cause conflict?
A: Different stakeholders want different outcomes, e.g. homeowners versus conservationists
